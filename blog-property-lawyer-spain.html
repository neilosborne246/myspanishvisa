Do You Need a Property Lawyer in Spain? What They Do and Why It Matters

Buying property in Spain as a foreigner without independent legal representation is one of the riskiest decisions you can make. Here is what a property lawyer actually does — and why they are worth every cent.

Home / Blog / Do You Need a Property Lawyer in Spain?

Spain's property market is genuinely attractive — competitive prices compared to many northern European markets, excellent quality of life, and a long-established culture of foreign ownership. But the legal landscape is very different from what buyers from the UK, US, Canada, Australia, or South Africa are used to. Without proper legal guidance, buyers can — and do — lose significant sums of money.

The most important thing to understand from the outset: the Spanish Notary Public is not your lawyer. The Notary's role is to witness the signing of the title deeds, verify the identities of both parties, and ensure the correct taxes are paid. They do not carry out legal due diligence on the property. That is entirely your responsibility — which means you need someone in your corner who is working exclusively for you.

What Does a Property Lawyer in Spain Actually Do?

A fully qualified Spanish property lawyer (called an abogado with expertise in derecho inmobiliario) does far more than review contracts. Here is what independent legal representation should include at every stage of your purchase:

1. Property Due Diligence

Before you commit a single euro, your lawyer should obtain and analyse the Nota Simple from the Land Registry. This document reveals the true legal owner, the exact description and boundaries of the property, and — critically — any mortgages, liens, or charges attached to it. In Spain, debts travel with the property rather than with the person who incurred them. Buying without this check means you could inherit someone else's unpaid mortgage, outstanding community fees, or local council tax arrears.

A thorough lawyer will also check the property's planning status with the local municipality, verify that any extensions or outbuildings have the correct planning permission, and confirm the property holds a valid Licence of First Occupation (Licencia de Primera Ocupación) — without which you cannot legally connect to utilities or use the property as a permanent home.

2. Reviewing and Negotiating Contracts

The first document you will be asked to sign is usually a reservation contract, which takes the property off the market while due diligence proceeds. Never sign this without legal review — standard estate agent reservation contracts frequently lack protective clauses for the buyer. Your lawyer should ensure the contract makes your deposit explicitly refundable if legal searches reveal serious problems.

The next key document is the Contrato de Arras Penitenciales — the private purchase agreement where you typically pay 10% of the purchase price. This contract establishes the completion deadline and the penalty terms: if the seller withdraws after this point, they must return double your deposit. Your lawyer should draft or heavily revise this contract to protect your position.

3. NIE Number and Bank Account Assistance

To buy property in Spain, you must have an NIE number (Número de Identidad de Extranjero). Your property lawyer can assist with the NIE application process and, where relevant, help you open a Spanish bank account — both of which are essential steps before you can complete the purchase.

4. Completion at the Notary

Your lawyer should attend the notary signing with you to review the final deed (Escritura de Compraventa) before you sign and ensure everything reflects what was agreed. If you cannot travel to Spain for the signing, you can grant your lawyer a Power of Attorney to complete the entire process on your behalf — a completely standard and legally secure arrangement.

5. Post-Completion Administration

After completion, there are taxes to pay (property transfer tax or VAT, plus stamp duty), the new title deed to register at the Land Registry, and utility contracts to transfer into your name. A full-service lawyer handles all of this so you can focus on enjoying your new home.

Why You Should Never Use the Estate Agent's Recommended Lawyer

Conflict of interest warning: It is very common in Spain for estate agents and property developers to recommend a "trusted" local lawyer to handle purchases. We strongly advise against accepting this recommendation. A lawyer who receives regular client referrals from an agent has a financial incentive to see the sale completed — even if due diligence reveals serious problems.

Your lawyer's sole duty must be to you. They should have no financial relationship whatsoever with the selling agent, the developer, or the bank arranging your mortgage. If a problem is discovered — illegal building work, unregistered land, outstanding debts — an independent lawyer will tell you clearly and help you walk away with your deposit protected. A lawyer beholden to the agent may not.

How to Verify a Spanish Property Lawyer's Credentials

In Spain, the property sector is largely unregulated. Anyone can describe themselves as a "legal advisor" or "property consultant" without holding a law degree or professional indemnity insurance. This matters enormously — if something goes wrong with your purchase and your advisor is not a qualified and registered abogado, you have no financial protection.

Always verify that your legal representative is registered with the Spanish Bar Association (Colegio de Abogados). You can search the national directory at abogacia.es. Registered abogados carry mandatory professional indemnity insurance, are bound by a strict code of ethics, and are subject to disciplinary procedures if they act improperly.

Five Property Traps That Only a Lawyer Can Protect You From

  • Illegal structures: Extensions, pools, or outbuildings built without planning permission are shockingly common, especially in rural and coastal areas. The local council can order demolition at the owner's expense — meaning your expense after you buy.
  • Hidden debts attached to the property: Outstanding mortgage balances, unpaid IBI (council tax), community fee arrears, or utility debts all pass to the new owner under Spanish law. Your lawyer checks for all of these before completion.
  • Under-declaration of price: Some sellers propose declaring a lower official sale price and receiving the balance in cash, reducing their capital gains tax liability. Participating in this arrangement is illegal and creates serious tax exposure for you when you eventually sell.
  • Unprotected reservation deposits: Without protective clauses in the reservation contract, losing your deposit to a collapsed sale or a seller who changes their mind is entirely possible.
  • Off-plan purchase risks: Buying new build from a developer before completion requires your lawyer to secure a bank guarantee (Aval Bancario). If the developer becomes insolvent or fails to complete, your deposit is legally protected and refundable.

How Much Does a Property Lawyer Cost in Spain?

Reputable property lawyers typically charge approximately 1% of the purchase price, subject to a minimum fee that varies by firm and complexity. On a €250,000 purchase, expect to pay around €2,500 plus Spanish VAT (IVA at 21%). This fee covers all the due diligence, contract review, notary attendance, tax payment, and Land Registry registration described above.

It is an investment that protects an asset worth hundreds of thousands of euros. The handful of buyers who skip legal representation to save a few thousand euros have sometimes lost everything.

Do I Still Need a Lawyer If I Am Buying with a Mortgage?

Yes. Your mortgage lender will carry out a valuation and assess their own security, but they are protecting their interest in the property — not yours. The bank's solicitor works for the bank. You still need your own independent lawyer conducting due diligence and protecting your position throughout the transaction.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a property lawyer legally required when buying in Spain?
No — using a lawyer is not a legal requirement in Spain. But it is the single most important thing you can do to protect your investment. The Notary Public witnesses signatures and confirms taxes are paid; they do not carry out legal due diligence on the property. That responsibility falls entirely on your own lawyer.
How much does a property lawyer cost in Spain?
Reputable property lawyers typically charge around 1% of the purchase price, subject to a minimum fee. For a property at €200,000, expect to pay approximately €2,000 plus VAT. Be cautious of unusually cheap quotes — unregulated advisors may not carry professional indemnity insurance, leaving you with no recourse if things go wrong.
Can I use the lawyer recommended by the estate agent?
We strongly advise against this. A lawyer who receives regular referrals from a real estate agency has a financial incentive to ensure the sale completes — even if problems are uncovered during due diligence. Your lawyer should be completely independent, with no financial relationship with the agent or developer.
What is the Nota Simple and why does it matter?
The Nota Simple is an extract from the Spanish Land Registry (Registro de la Propiedad). It shows the property's legal owner, the exact boundaries and description, and any charges or mortgages attached to it. In Spain, debts are attached to the property rather than the person — so buying without checking the Nota Simple means you could inherit the previous owner's unpaid mortgage or council tax debts.
Can my lawyer handle everything if I cannot travel to Spain?
Yes. By granting your lawyer a Power of Attorney (Poder Notarial), they can carry out every step of the purchase on your behalf — from due diligence and negotiations through to signing at the Notary. This is a common arrangement for overseas buyers and is completely legal and secure.

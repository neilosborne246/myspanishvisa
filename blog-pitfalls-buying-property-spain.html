Pitfalls of Buying Property in Spain: 8 Mistakes Foreign Buyers Make

Spain is an outstanding place to buy property — but the legal and administrative landscape is different from what buyers are used to in the UK, US, Canada or Australia. These are the mistakes we see most often, and how to avoid every one of them.

Spain operates on a principle of buyer beware. Unlike many common-law jurisdictions where automatic legal protections exist during a property transaction, Spain places a significant burden of due diligence on the purchaser. If you sign a contract on a property with hidden debts, an illegal swimming pool or an unresolved planning dispute, those problems become yours on the date you complete. The good news is that every single pitfall below is entirely avoidable — provided you go in with the right advice and the right professional team.

Pitfall 1: Not Obtaining Your NIE Before You Start

The NIE (Número de Identificación de Extranjero) is Spain's foreigner identification number, and you cannot complete a property purchase without one. You need an NIE to sign any purchase deed, to open a Spanish bank account, to apply for a mortgage and to pay the taxes associated with the purchase. Many buyers only discover this requirement weeks or months into their property search, after they have already found a property and made an offer. The resulting scramble to obtain an NIE at short notice can delay a purchase, irritate a motivated seller, or even cause you to miss a preferred completion date.

Apply for your NIE early — either at the Spanish consulate in your home country or at a police station in Spain — and treat it as the very first step of your property journey, not something to sort out later.

Pitfall 2: Skipping the Nota Simple

The nota simple is a short official extract from the Spanish Land Registry (Registro de la Propiedad). It confirms who legally owns the property, its registered boundaries and dimensions, and — critically — whether any charges, mortgages, embargoes or other encumbrances are registered against it. In Spain, debts attach to the property rather than to the person who incurred them. If a seller owes €20,000 on an outstanding mortgage, that debt transfers to you on completion unless it is cleared beforehand.

A nota simple costs between €8 and €20 and takes minutes to obtain. There is no excuse for not having one on every property you are seriously considering. A good property lawyer will obtain and review this document as the very first step of their due diligence.

Pitfall 3: Inheriting Unpaid IBI and Community Fee Debts

IBI (Impuesto sobre Bienes Inmuebles) is Spain's annual council tax. Unpaid IBI creates a statutory charge against the property itself, which passes to the new owner on completion. Community fees work the same way: if the previous owner owes three years of unpaid charges to the residents' community (comunidad de propietarios), the new owner becomes jointly liable for that debt under Spain's Horizontal Property Law.

Before completing any purchase, request a certificate from the seller confirming that IBI payments are up to date, and ask the community administrator for a certificate of no arrears (certificado de estar al corriente). Your lawyer should check these documents, not simply accept the seller's verbal assurance.

Pitfall 4: Buying Without Checking for Illegal Structures

Spain has a substantial history of unlicensed construction, particularly in coastal and rural areas where property booms outpaced planning oversight. Swimming pools, extensions, guest houses and garages added without planning permission are common. In some regions, entire developments were built on land that was never legally zoned for residential use.

The danger is not merely aesthetic. Spanish planning authorities can issue demolition orders for unlicensed structures, and these orders are not time-limited in the way they are in some other jurisdictions. Before you buy, your lawyer should verify with the local town hall (ayuntamiento) that the property has a valid habitation licence (cédula de habitabilidad) and that all structures on the plot correspond to those shown on the cadastral plans. Any discrepancy is a red flag that requires resolution before you sign.

Pitfall 5: Rural Land Classification Surprises

Spanish land is classified as either urban (suelo urbano), developable (suelo urbanizable) or protected rural land (suelo no urbanizable). The classification has a profound effect on what you can legally build, extend or modify on a property. Properties marketed as charming rural fincas are occasionally situated on protected land where even basic renovations require special permits, where connecting to mains services may be impossible, or where future sale to certain types of buyer may be restricted.

If you are considering a rural or semi-rural property, have your lawyer check the land classification with both the cadastre and the local planning authority before you commit to anything.

Pitfall 6: Off-Plan Purchases Without Stage-Payment Guarantees

Buying a property that has not yet been built — known in Spain as off-plan or sobre plano — can offer attractive prices, but it carries distinct risks. Developer insolvencies, construction delays and specification changes have all affected foreign buyers in Spain over the years. The key legal protection is a bank guarantee (aval bancario) or insurance policy that covers every stage payment you make during construction. Spanish law requires developers to provide this protection, but buyers who do not know to ask for it — or who sign contracts without having them reviewed — sometimes discover it is missing.

Any off-plan purchase should be reviewed by an independent lawyer before you pay a penny. The stage payment schedule, completion guarantee, penalty clauses and the developer's financial standing all need to be checked.

Pitfall 7: Using the Seller's Lawyer

It is surprisingly common for foreign buyers to be guided, by an estate agent or even a developer, towards using the seller's recommended legal advisor. This is a significant conflict of interest. A lawyer acting for the seller owes duties to that client, not to you. They are unlikely to tell you about structural problems, unpaid debts or planning issues that might cause you to renegotiate or walk away from the deal.

Always instruct your own independent lawyer — one who has no commercial relationship with the seller, the developer or the estate agent. Your lawyer's only loyalty should be to you and to protecting your interests in the transaction.

Pitfall 8: Assuming Property Ownership Grants Residency

This is perhaps the most significant misconception we encounter among clients from the UK, US, Canada, Australia and South Africa. Owning a property in Spain — regardless of its value — does not entitle you to live there for more than 90 days in any 180-day period. The Schengen rules apply to non-EU nationals whether or not they own real estate in the zone.

If you intend to spend significant time in Spain — or to retire there permanently — you need a Spanish residency visa in addition to your property. The Non-Lucrative Visa is the most popular route for those with pensions or investment income, while the Digital Nomad Visa suits remote workers and freelancers. Both routes are manageable, fully English-language processes, and we can handle your visa application and property purchase simultaneously to make the transition as smooth as possible.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a nota simple and why do I need one?
A nota simple is a short extract from the Spanish Land Registry showing who owns the property, its legal boundaries, and any charges, mortgages or debts registered against it. You should obtain one for any property you are seriously considering, before paying any deposit. It costs €8–€20 and can be obtained within hours.
Can I buy property in Spain without an NIE?
No. An NIE is legally required to sign any property purchase deed, open a Spanish bank account or pay property taxes. Apply for your NIE as the very first step of your property search — not something to leave until you have found a property.
What happens if I buy a property with an illegal extension in Spain?
You inherit the problem. Spanish planning authorities can issue demolition orders for unlicensed structures regardless of who built them or when the property last changed hands. Always verify that all structures shown on a property have the correct planning permissions and habitation licences before signing.
What is IBI and should I check it before buying?
IBI (Impuesto sobre Bienes Inmuebles) is Spain's annual council tax charged on property ownership. Unpaid IBI creates a charge against the property that transfers to the new owner. Always request a certificate confirming IBI is fully up to date before completing a purchase.
Does buying a property in Spain give me the right to live there?
No. Property ownership does not grant residency rights. Non-EU nationals — including UK citizens since Brexit — are limited to 90 days in any 180-day period in the Schengen Area unless they hold a valid Spanish residency visa such as the Non-Lucrative Visa or Digital Nomad Visa.

Living in Marbella as an Expat: Lifestyle, Costs, and What to Expect

The Instagram version of Marbella is all superyachts and pool parties at Puerto Banús. The version most expats actually live — walking a dog along the seafront before 9am, picking up vegetables at the market in the Old Town, playing golf on a Tuesday morning — is considerably more ordinary, and considerably more rewarding for it.

Marbella has a reputation problem — not because the reality is bad, but because the image so thoroughly dominates the conversation. Say "Marbella" to most people and they picture fur coats in August, bottle service in nightclubs, and Russian oligarchs moored in the marina. The reality of daily expat life here is far more grounded, and far more appealing to the people who actually stay.

The Marbella that thousands of British, German, Scandinavian, and American expats have made home over the past thirty years is a place of exceptional climate, reliable private healthcare, a well-established international community, and a quality of outdoor living — golf, beaches, hiking in the Sierra Blanca — that is genuinely difficult to match at this price point in western Europe.

This guide is about that Marbella. The one people actually live in.

Why Marbella Draws Expats — and What It's Really Like

The honest answer to "why Marbella?" usually comes down to three things: the climate, the community, and the lifestyle infrastructure. Marbella gets over 320 days of sunshine per year. Winters are mild — temperatures rarely drop below 10°C even in January, and a winter's day of 17°C with blue sky is common. Coming from the UK, Ireland, or Scandinavia, this alone is life-changing.

The international community is large enough to feel like genuine support rather than an expat bubble. You will find English-speaking doctors, solicitors, estate agents, accountants, and hairdressers without difficulty. There are English-language community groups, international schools, and churches. This matters enormously in the early months, when you are navigating a new bureaucratic system in a foreign language.

The lifestyle infrastructure — the golf courses, the beach clubs, the restaurants, the hiking trails — is real and accessible without necessarily being expensive. You can spend €200 on dinner at a beach club or €15 at a tapas bar in the Old Town. Both exist. The assumption that Marbella forces you into a specific kind of consumption is wrong. It forces nothing. What it offers is options.

What it does not offer is anonymity from its own reputation. If you tell people you live in Marbella, some will picture a certain lifestyle. Most actual residents find this mildly amusing and entirely irrelevant to their daily lives.

Areas of Marbella: Which Part Is Right for You?

Old Town (Casco Antiguo)

The historical heart of Marbella is genuinely lovely: whitewashed walls, terracotta pots of geraniums, the Plaza de los Naranjos (Orange Tree Square) lined with outdoor restaurants, narrow lanes that open unexpectedly onto churches or fountains. It has a distinctly Spanish character that the marina areas lack entirely.

The Old Town is walkable, with good local shops, a covered market, and restaurants at every price point. For expats who want to integrate into local Spanish life rather than an international bubble, it is the best starting point. Apartments here are smaller and older than elsewhere, and parking is difficult — but if you are happy without a car and want character over convenience, the Old Town delivers.

Puerto Banús

The marina is exactly what it looks like from the photographs: superyachts, Ferraris on the promenade, designer boutiques, and beach clubs charging €30 for a gin and tonic. It is spectacular to visit and genuinely exhausting to live next to year-round, particularly in summer when the traffic and crowds can make basic errands feel like an expedition.

Puerto Banús suits a specific type of resident — someone who actively wants to be at the centre of that scene, who travels frequently and treats their Marbella base as a high-end pied-à-terre. For permanent residents wanting a functioning daily life, it is not the most practical choice. The surrounding residential areas (particularly near the beach behind the marina) are more liveable than the marina itself.

The Golden Mile

The stretch of coastline and luxury villas between central Marbella and Puerto Banús is known as the Golden Mile, and it is where some of the most expensive real estate in Spain sits. Grand gated villas, five-star hotels (the Marbella Club, Puente Romano), and sea-facing estates. This is aspirational Marbella at its most concentrated.

Few expats who are not genuinely wealthy live here — the rental prices for even a modest villa are significant, and the lifestyle expectation of the area carries its own costs. If this is your world, it is extraordinary. If it is not, there are better-value options nearby that still deliver excellent quality of life.

Nueva Andalucía

This is where the majority of longer-term expat residents actually live, and for good reason. Situated just behind Puerto Banús and the golf valley, Nueva Andalucía offers significantly better value than the coastal strip while remaining minutes from the beach. Apartment complexes with pools are the norm — you can rent a well-appointed two-bedroom apartment in a gated community with a pool for €1,200–€1,800 per month, significantly less than an equivalent flat in central London or Dublin.

The area is home to several of the Costa del Sol's most famous golf clubs (including Las Brisas and Aloha), making it particularly popular with golf-focused retirees. It has its own supermarkets, restaurants, and services — a functional residential area that happens to be five minutes from one of the world's most famous marinas.

San Pedro de Alcántara

San Pedro, to the west of central Marbella, is the area that most closely resembles a normal Spanish town. A proper high street, local bars with Spanish clientele, a weekly market, authentic restaurants where you can eat a three-course menu del día for €12. If you want to live in Spain rather than in an international enclave, San Pedro is worth serious consideration.

It is also cheaper than anywhere else in the Marbella municipality. Rents run roughly 20–30% lower than the central areas, and the quality of life for day-to-day living is high. The beach is accessible (the Playa de San Pedro is long and uncrowded outside summer), and the old town is genuinely pleasant. The trade-off is less of the international infrastructure that newer arrivals tend to rely on.

Cost of Living in Marbella: Real Numbers

Marbella is more expensive than most of Andalusia — more than Malaga city, Seville, or Granada. It is less expensive than Madrid or Barcelona. Here is what a realistic monthly budget looks like in 2026:

Entry-level lifestyle (apartment in Nueva Andalucía or San Pedro, basic car)

  • Rent, one-bedroom apartment: €900–€1,200
  • Groceries (Mercadona/Lidl): €250–€320
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet): €110–€160
  • Private health insurance (age 60–70): €100–€200 per month
  • Car (insurance, fuel, maintenance): €200–€300
  • Eating out, leisure: €250–€400
  • Total: approximately €1,810–€2,580 per month

Comfortable lifestyle (central area or good Nueva Andalucía complex, regular dining out)

  • Rent, two-bedroom apartment with pool: €1,400–€2,000
  • Groceries and dining out combined: €600–€900
  • Utilities: €140–€200
  • Private health insurance: €150–€250
  • Car: €250–€400
  • Golf (membership or green fees), leisure, culture: €400–€700
  • Total: approximately €2,940–€4,450 per month

Property prices in Marbella are high but not at London or Monaco levels. Two-bedroom apartments in Nueva Andalucía sell for €300,000–€600,000; villas on the Golden Mile for €2m–€10m+. For renters, the market is competitive — popular complexes fill quickly, and summer short-term lets shrink the available long-term stock.

Healthcare in Marbella

Healthcare is one of Marbella's genuine strengths for expats. The anchor facility is Hospital Vithas Xanit Internacional in Benalmádena (a short drive from Marbella), one of the most highly regarded private hospitals on the Costa del Sol, with English-speaking staff and experience with international patients and health insurance. It covers everything from routine consultations to major surgery.

For public healthcare, Hospital Costa del Sol in Marbella serves the area's NHS (Sistema Nacional de Salud). Non-EU residents on a Non-Lucrative Visa must hold private health insurance as a visa requirement — but even those with public access often choose to use private facilities for speed and language comfort.

Private health insurance in Spain costs considerably less than UK private health insurance and a fraction of US coverage. A comprehensive policy for a healthy 65-year-old runs €150–€300 per month depending on provider, age, and cover level. Spanish insurers including Sanitas, Adeslas, Asisa, and international providers such as AXA and Cigna all operate in the area.

Getting Around: A Car Is Not Optional

This is one of the most practically important things to understand about Marbella: outside the walkable confines of the Old Town, you will need a car. The municipality stretches from San Pedro in the west to the boundaries of Ojén in the hills, and public transport does not knit it together effectively.

Buses connect the main areas along the coastal road, but services are slow, infrequent, and impractical for daily errands. There is no train line. Taxis and Cabify/Uber are available but expensive for daily use. A car — ideally purchased or leased in Spain once you establish residency — gives you genuine freedom: access to the golf courses, the beaches, the hiking trails in the Serranía de Ronda, Malaga city for a day out, and the day-to-day practicalities of supermarkets, medical appointments, and administrative tasks.

Driving standards in Spain are broadly similar to northern Europe. Roads are generally in good condition. Parking in the Old Town is the main frustration — underground car parks exist but fill quickly in summer.

Social Scene and the Expat Community

The Marbella expat community is large, diverse, and well-organised. British expats form the largest English-speaking group, but there are significant German, Scandinavian, Russian, and American communities, along with long-established communities from Saudi Arabia and the Gulf states. The diversity is part of what makes Marbella interesting — it is genuinely international in a way that a small town in rural Andalusia is not.

Social infrastructure is well-developed. International Women's Club Marbella, the British Society of Marbella, golf societies, expat networking groups, charity committees, and various language exchange groups provide abundant social entry points for new arrivals. The calendar is busy year-round — events at the beach clubs, charity galas, cultural evenings, golf tournaments.

One thing worth saying honestly: Marbella expat social life can default towards a particular demographic (older, property-wealthy, retired). If you are younger, a remote worker, or looking for a different kind of social scene, you will need to be more deliberate about finding your people — but they are there. Coworking spaces in central Marbella attract a growing community of digital nomads and remote workers, and the café culture in the Old Town supports a more varied social ecosystem than the golf club circuit.

Climate: The Real Reason People Stay

Marbella's climate is genuinely exceptional by European standards. Sheltered by the Sierra Blanca mountains to the north, it receives less rainfall than almost anywhere else on the Spanish mainland. Summers are warm and sunny (July/August averaging around 28–31°C, sea temperature reaching 25°C). Winters are mild — a typical January day is 15°C with blue sky. Frosts are almost unknown at sea level.

The shoulder seasons — April to June and September to November — are arguably the best time to be here: warm enough for the beach, calm enough to actually enjoy it without the summer crowds. Many long-term expats regard October and November as the finest months of the year.

For British residents particularly, the contrast with home is stark and permanent. People who have spent decades in grey northern winters describe the Marbella climate as transformative — not just physically, but psychologically. It is not a trivial consideration.

Bureaucracy: Registering Your Residency in Málaga Province

Once you arrive in Marbella on your residence visa, the main bureaucratic tasks are registering on the padrón (municipal register) at Marbella Town Hall, and applying for your TIE (Tarjeta de Identidad de Extranjero — foreigner's identity card) at the Foreigners Office (Oficina de Extranjería) in Málaga city.

Málaga is the provincial capital for this area, and many residency-related bureaucratic appointments must be made there rather than in Marbella itself. The journey is around 55 kilometres — manageable by car or the regular bus service from Marbella's bus station. Appointment slots at the Málaga foreigners office can be competitive; book well in advance through the official system.

Your NIE (Número de Identificación de Extranjero) is required for almost every significant financial or legal transaction in Spain: opening a bank account, signing a rental contract, registering a car, or purchasing property. Obtaining it should be one of your first priorities.

Working with an experienced immigration specialist significantly reduces the friction of this process, particularly for the initial visa application and the subsequent TIE registration. Small errors — the wrong document format, a missing apostille, an incorrect translation — can cause meaningful delays.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Marbella a good place to live as an expat?
Yes — for the right person. Marbella offers an exceptional climate (320+ days of sunshine), a large and established English-speaking expat community, excellent private healthcare, and a quality of outdoor lifestyle that is hard to match anywhere in Europe at a comparable price. It is more expensive than most Andalusian cities, and a car is essential for living comfortably outside the central area. But for retirees and remote workers who value warmth, sun, and Mediterranean living, it consistently delivers.
How much does it cost to live in Marbella?
A single person can live comfortably in Marbella for €1,800–€2,500 per month, covering rent, food, utilities, private health insurance, and leisure. A couple sharing costs can expect €2,800–€3,800 per month for a comfortable lifestyle. Renting in Nueva Andalucía or San Pedro is significantly cheaper than central Marbella or near Puerto Banús — a one-bedroom apartment ranges from €900 in outer areas to €1,500+ in prime locations.
Which area of Marbella is best for expats?
It depends on your lifestyle and budget. The Old Town is walkable, characterful, and great for those who want a Spanish feel without a car. Nueva Andalucía offers the best value for money — good-quality apartments at lower prices, close to golf and Puerto Banús. San Pedro de Alcántara has a genuinely local feel and is popular with longer-term expats. Puerto Banús and the Golden Mile are for those with the budget to match the postcode.
Do I need a car in Marbella?
Almost certainly, yes. Marbella's central Old Town is walkable, but the wider municipality is sprawling and poorly served by public transport. Most expats find a car essential for day-to-day life, including grocery shopping, reaching healthcare facilities, and accessing the golf courses and beaches that make Marbella worth living in.
What visa do I need to live in Marbella as a non-EU citizen?
Non-EU citizens need a Spanish residence visa for long-term stays. For retirees and those living on passive income, the Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) is the standard route. For those who work remotely for non-Spanish employers, Spain's Digital Nomad Visa (DNV) is the appropriate option. Both require application through your Spanish consulate before you move.
Is Marbella expensive compared to the rest of Spain?
Yes — Marbella is among the pricier destinations in Spain, particularly for rental property. It is more expensive than Malaga city, Seville, or Alicante. That said, it remains considerably cheaper than London, Dublin, Sydney, or Zurich — and the lifestyle payoff in terms of climate, quality of life, and outdoor living is real. You pay a premium for the Marbella address, but you are not paying Monaco prices.

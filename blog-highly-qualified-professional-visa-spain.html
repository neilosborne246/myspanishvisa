Spain's Highly Qualified Professional Visa (HQP): The Corporate Relocation Route

Multinationals sending senior staff to Spain, and Spanish companies hiring talent from abroad, have a dedicated visa route that bypasses the standard quota system entirely — and turns around decisions in 20 working days. Here is exactly how it works.

Spain's immigration system has a well-deserved reputation for complexity, but for the specific group of people this visa is designed for — senior managers, specialists, and highly qualified professionals moving to Spain under a corporate umbrella — the process is actually more organised than most people realise. The legislation is clear, the processing unit is dedicated, and the timeline is fixed. The challenge is not the system itself. It is navigating the requirements correctly the first time.

The Highly Qualified Professional Visa (known in Spanish as the visa para profesionales altamente cualificados, or PAC visa) sits within the framework of Spain's Law 14/2013 — the Ley de Emprendedores, or Entrepreneurs' Law — which created a suite of fast-track residence and work authorisations designed to attract international talent and investment. This is not the startup visa or the Digital Nomad Visa. It is specifically for employees: people with an employment contract, a salary, and a company standing behind their application.

Who this is not for: The HQP Visa does not apply to freelancers, self-employed professionals, or anyone working independently. If you plan to work for your own clients from Spain, the Digital Nomad Visa is the relevant route. Compare all visa options here.

The Two Pathways: Corporate Transfer vs Direct Hire

The HQP Visa covers two distinct situations, and it is worth being precise about which applies to you — because the documentation requirements differ.

Intra-company transfer (corporate relocation)

This is the most common use case. A multinational with operations in Spain (a subsidiary, branch, or representative office) wants to relocate one of its foreign employees to the Spanish entity. The employee remains employed by the group — their contract may be with the parent company or novated to the Spanish entity — and the transfer must be genuine, meaning there must be an actual operational reason for the move. Senior managers, directors, and technical specialists with expertise that is genuinely needed in Spain all qualify. The employee must have been employed by the group for a minimum of three months before the application.

Direct hire by a Spanish company

A Spanish employer wants to recruit a non-EU professional for a role that meets the "highly qualified" threshold. Unlike the standard work permit system, this route does not require the employer to prove that no suitable Spanish or EU candidate exists (the so-called "national employment situation" test). That exemption alone makes the HQP pathway enormously more practical for employers trying to hire specialist talent internationally.

In both cases the employer — not the employee — initiates the authorisation. The employee cannot apply independently. This is a corporate tool.

Salary Requirements: What "Highly Qualified" Actually Means

Salary is the clearest quantitative test in the HQP framework, though it is applied differently depending on the pathway.

For intra-company transfers of managers and senior employees, the law does not set a single fixed minimum. The salary must be equivalent to what a comparable Spanish employee in the same role would earn. In practice this means the application will be assessed against market benchmarks for the sector and seniority level. For most management roles this threshold comfortably exceeds €50,000 per year, and for executive-level positions it is typically substantially higher.

For highly qualified professionals hired directly, the benchmark is more explicit: the salary should be at least 1.5 times the average salary in the relevant occupational group or sector. Depending on the sector, this generally places the practical minimum somewhere between €40,000 and €54,000 per year in 2026. Technology, finance, engineering, and life sciences roles tend to sit at the higher end of that range.

The assessment is qualitative as much as quantitative. A salary that looks borderline on paper can still succeed if the role is credibly senior and the employer's documentation is compelling. Conversely, a generous-looking salary attached to a vaguely described role can attract scrutiny. How the application is constructed matters.

The UGE-CE Route: Spain's Fast-Track for Major Employers

Spain's standard work permit system operates through provincial immigration offices, is subject to annual national employment quotas, and can take months. The UGE-CE — Unidad de Grandes Empresas y Colectivos Estratégicos — is an entirely separate processing unit, based in Madrid, dedicated to applications from large companies and strategically important employers. For qualifying organisations, it changes the equation entirely.

The UGE-CE handles all HQP Visa applications, regardless of where in Spain the employee will be based. It has a processing target of 20 working days from receipt of a complete application. In practice this is largely met. For a multinational with HR deadlines and relocation timelines to manage, this predictability is genuinely valuable.

Does your company qualify for UGE-CE?

The UGE-CE route is available to companies that meet at least one of the following criteria:

  • Annual turnover exceeding €100 million
  • Significant foreign investment in Spain
  • Substantial workforce in Spain (typically 500 or more employees)
  • Companies operating in strategically important sectors as defined by the Spanish government

Smaller companies and direct-hire cases where the employer does not meet these thresholds can still apply for HQP authorisation through the standard route — it simply takes longer and goes through regional offices. The outcome is the same; the timeline is not.

How the Standard Work Permit Compares

The contrast with the conventional Spanish work permit is stark and worth spelling out, because many HR departments approaching a Spain relocation for the first time do not realise there is an alternative.

The standard work permit requires the employer to demonstrate that no suitable EU candidate is available for the role (the national employment situation test). This involves advertising the position through Spain's employment service (SEPE) and waiting for confirmation. The process runs through regional immigration offices with varying workloads and processing times, often stretching to four to six months or longer. It is subject to annual quota limits on certain categories of worker.

The HQP Visa bypasses the employment situation test entirely, uses the centralised UGE-CE unit with a fixed 20-working-day target, and operates outside the quota system. For any company that qualifies, choosing the standard route when the HQP route is available would be a significant procedural mistake.

What the Employer Must Do

The employer carries the weight of this application. The employee's role is to provide personal documents; the employer's role is to make the substantive legal case.

Key employer obligations include:

  • Being registered and up to date with Spain's Social Security system
  • Providing a formal letter of appointment or contract specifying the role, salary, duration, and location
  • Demonstrating the company's credentials (incorporation documents, accounts, evidence of Spanish operations)
  • For corporate transfers: evidence of the group relationship between the sending entity and the Spanish entity
  • For UGE-CE applications: confirmation that the company meets the UGE-CE registration criteria
  • Registering the employee with the Spanish Social Security system once the authorisation is granted

The application is submitted in Spain by the employer or their legal representative. The employee does not need to be in Spain at this stage — they remain in their home country while the Spanish authorisation is processed. Once the work authorisation is granted, the employee then applies for the visa at the Spanish consulate in their home country, a process that typically takes two to four weeks.

Processing Timeline: The Full Picture

The 20-working-day UGE-CE target is real, but it covers only one part of the journey. A realistic end-to-end timeline looks like this:

  • Document preparation and employer registration: 2–4 weeks (variable; depends on how quickly the company can gather its corporate documentation)
  • UGE-CE processing of work authorisation: approximately 20 working days (4–5 weeks)
  • Consulate visa application and appointment: 2–4 weeks
  • Employee travels to Spain, registers with Social Security, obtains TIE residence card: 2–3 weeks after arrival

Total elapsed time from starting document preparation to the employee being fully settled in Spain with a valid residence card: typically 10 to 14 weeks. Companies that have done this before and have their employer documentation in order can compress that window significantly.

Bringing Your Family

Family reunification is an integral part of the HQP route, not an afterthought. A spouse or registered partner and dependent children can apply to join the primary visa holder in Spain, either simultaneously or shortly after the main authorisation is granted.

Spouses of HQP Visa holders have a notable advantage: they are generally entitled to work in Spain without needing a separate work authorisation. This is explicitly more generous than the standard family reunification rules that apply under ordinary work permits, where a spouse typically cannot work until they have held legal residence for a set period. For dual-career households, this matters a great deal.

Children attend Spanish state schools on the same basis as Spanish children. Private international schools are available in most major cities for families who prefer English-medium education.

Path to Permanent Residency and Citizenship

The HQP authorisation is initially granted for one to three years depending on the nature of the contract, and it is renewable. After five years of continuous legal residence in Spain, the holder becomes eligible to apply for long-term EU residency status — a significantly more secure form of residency that is not tied to employment.

Spanish citizenship is available after ten years of legal residence for most nationalities, with shorter periods for nationals of Latin American countries, the Philippines, Equatorial Guinea, and Andorra (two years), and Sephardic Jews under a separate legislation. Spain allows dual nationality with a number of countries — worth checking your specific nationality's treaty status before applying.

One important consideration: to maintain eligibility for permanent residency, absences from Spain must not exceed certain thresholds. Frequent long-haul travel — common for executives — can disrupt the continuous residence calculation if not managed carefully. A specialist can advise on how to structure absences to protect your residency timeline.

The Beckham Law: The Tax Angle That Changes the Calculation

For many senior professionals, the tax treatment of a Spain relocation is as important as the visa itself. Spain's special expatriate tax regime — formally the Régimen Especial para Trabajadores Desplazados, universally known as the Beckham Law after the footballer whose 2003 move to Real Madrid made it famous — applies directly to HQP Visa holders and deserves serious attention.

The headline benefit: instead of being taxed as a Spanish resident under the standard progressive schedule (which reaches 47% at the top), qualifying workers pay a flat 24% rate on Spanish-sourced income up to €600,000 per year. Above that threshold, the 47% rate applies — but for the vast majority of relocated professionals, this structure represents a substantial saving compared with what they would pay as a full Spanish tax resident.

The regime applies for the tax year of arrival plus the following five years — six years in total. To qualify, the individual must not have been a Spanish tax resident at any point during the five years before their arrival. The election must be made within six months of first registering with the Spanish Social Security system. Miss that window and the opportunity is gone for that particular relocation — there is no retroactive application.

Spain's 2022 Startup Law extended the Beckham Law to cover the Digital Nomad Visa, which increased awareness of the regime significantly. But HQP Visa holders have always been among its primary intended beneficiaries, and the combination of a fast-track authorisation and a six-year preferential tax rate makes Spain's corporate relocation route genuinely competitive internationally.

Important: The Beckham Law election and its ongoing administration require specialist tax advice. The interaction with home-country tax obligations, double taxation treaties, and investment income treatment is complex. The figures here are for orientation only — individual circumstances vary considerably.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Highly Qualified Professional Visa in Spain?
The Highly Qualified Professional Visa (HQP Visa, also called the PAC Visa) is a work authorisation created under Spain's Law 14/2013 (Ley de Emprendedores). It covers two groups: employees being relocated to Spain by a multinational company (intra-company transfer), and highly qualified professionals hired directly by a Spanish company. It is not available to the self-employed or freelancers. Processing runs through the UGE-CE unit, which handles applications in approximately 20 working days.
What salary do I need for the HQP Visa?
For intra-company transfers of managers and senior employees, there is no fixed statutory minimum — the salary must be comparable to what a Spanish employee in the same role would earn. For directly hired highly qualified professionals, the benchmark is typically at least 1.5 times the average salary in the relevant sector, which in practice generally means a minimum of €40,000–€54,000 per year depending on industry and role level. The assessment is qualitative as well as numerical, so how the application is structured matters as much as the raw figure.
Can my family come with me on the HQP Visa?
Yes. A spouse or registered partner and dependent children can apply for family reunification authorisation to accompany the primary HQP Visa holder to Spain. In most cases, spouses of HQP holders are also permitted to work in Spain without requiring a separate work authorisation — a significant advantage over the family reunification rules that apply under standard work permits.
What is the UGE-CE and how does it help my application?
The UGE-CE (Unidad de Grandes Empresas y Colectivos Estratégicos) is a dedicated unit within Spain's immigration system that processes applications from large companies and strategically important employers. Once a company is registered with the UGE-CE, its employee applications are handled by a specialist team with a processing target of 20 working days — considerably faster than the standard work permit quota system, which can take several months. For multinationals relocating staff to Spain, the UGE-CE route is almost always the correct pathway.
Does the HQP Visa qualify for the Beckham Law tax regime?
Yes, in most cases. HQP Visa holders who were not Spanish tax residents during the five years prior to arrival can elect to be taxed under Spain's special expatriate tax regime (the Beckham Law). This applies a flat 24% income tax rate on Spanish-sourced earnings up to €600,000, instead of Spain's standard progressive rates which reach 47% at the top. The regime applies for the year of arrival plus the following five years. The election must be made within six months of registering with the Spanish Social Security system.
How long does the HQP Visa take to process?
Applications processed through the UGE-CE fast-track route carry a target of 20 working days from submission of a complete file. Once the work authorisation is granted in Spain, the employee applies for a visa at their home country's Spanish consulate, which typically takes a further 2–4 weeks. Total elapsed time from starting document preparation to arriving in Spain is usually 10–14 weeks, depending on how quickly the employer's documentation is assembled and consulate appointment availability.

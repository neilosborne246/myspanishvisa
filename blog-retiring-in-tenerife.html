Retiring in Tenerife on the Non-Lucrative Visa: The 2026 Guide

Tenerife is home to one of Europe's largest retirement expat communities. This is the practical guide for people who are seriously considering it — with real numbers and an honest picture of what life here actually looks like.

Tenerife is home to one of Europe's largest retirement expat communities. Tens of thousands of British, German, Irish, and other northern European retirees have built their lives here — not as tourists passing through, but as permanent residents with established routines, social networks, and a genuine sense of home. The combination of near-perfect winter climate, affordable living costs relative to northern Europe, excellent private healthcare, and a deep-rooted English-speaking community makes the logistics of retirement easier here than almost anywhere else in Spain.

This guide is written for people who are seriously thinking about it. Not dreaming about it vaguely — actually considering the practicalities of what it costs, where to live, how healthcare works, and what the Non-Lucrative Visa requires. We have tried to give you the honest picture rather than the brochure version.

Why Retirees Choose Tenerife

The appeal is not one thing — it is a combination of factors that, taken together, make Tenerife unusually well-suited to retirement for non-EU nationals.

  • Year-round warmth: 18–24°C even in winter in the south — the core appeal for anyone who has spent decades under northern European skies
  • English-speaking infrastructure in the resort south that removes the language barrier for those who want it
  • One of Spain's most affordable living environments relative to quality of life — Canary Islands IGIC tax at 7% (versus 21% mainland VAT) keeps costs down structurally
  • Strong, established social scene: active expat clubs covering golf, bowls, walking, bridge, amateur dramatics, English-language churches, charity networks, organised excursions
  • Excellent private hospital access — Hospiten and Vithas chains across the south, Hospital Universitario de Canarias in La Laguna for complex cases
  • Flight connectivity to the UK from Tenerife Sur (TFS) that is both frequent and cheaper than many mainland Spanish city connections
  • No punishing summer heat: unlike Seville or Malaga, Tenerife rarely exceeds 28°C even in August — a genuine quality-of-life advantage for those who struggle with extreme heat

Monthly Budget Breakdown for Retirees in Tenerife (2026)

These are honest estimates based on actual costs. Individual circumstances vary, but this gives you a realistic working framework.

South Tenerife (Costa Adeje / Los Cristianos) — single retiree

  • 1-bedroom apartment: €750–€1,000/month
  • Food and groceries: €250–€350/month
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet): €90–€130/month
  • Private health insurance (age 60–70): €120–€200/month
  • Transport (car running costs or taxis): €150–€200/month
  • Eating out and leisure: €300–€500/month
  • Total: approximately €1,660–€2,380/month

North Tenerife (Puerto de la Cruz) — single retiree

  • 1-bedroom apartment: €600–€850/month
  • Food and groceries: €220–€300/month
  • Utilities: €80–€120/month
  • Private health insurance: €120–€200/month
  • Transport: €120–€180/month
  • Leisure and eating out: €250–€400/month
  • Total: approximately €1,390–€2,050/month

Couples sharing accommodation and fixed costs can reduce per-person spend significantly — splitting rent and utilities typically brings the combined household figure well below double the single-person figure.

One important note: the Non-Lucrative Visa income requirement (approximately €2,400/month for a single applicant in 2026, around €28,800/year) is a minimum threshold for the visa, not a spending target. Many retirees live comfortably on less than this after arriving.

Best Areas for Retirees in Tenerife

Costa Adeje

The upscale southern choice. Manicured resort infrastructure, excellent restaurants, golf courses, a well-developed marina, and newer residential developments at a good standard. The most expensive of the southern options, but the quality of facilities and environment justifies it for retirees who want comfort and resort-level amenities as part of daily life. Popular with British retirees who want the best of what Tenerife's south offers.

Los Cristianos

More down-to-earth than Costa Adeje, longer-established, and genuinely better value for money. The expat community here is deep-rooted and well-organised — walking groups, bowls clubs, social events, a lively weekly market. A real beach. A working harbour that gives the town human character beyond resort infrastructure. Most permanent retirees in the south who want community over polish end up in Los Cristianos.

Playa de las Américas

Lively and well-located but too tourist-heavy and noisy for most retirees seeking a settled, comfortable daily life. Worth considering if the buzz of a busy resort suits you, but most people looking for long-term retirement quality of life gravitate to Costa Adeje or Los Cristianos instead.

Puerto de la Cruz (north)

The alternative for retirees who want a more authentically Spanish retirement experience. An older town with a beautiful historic centre, botanical gardens, lush hillside vegetation, cooler temperatures, and a genuine local Spanish life alongside the expat community. Lower costs than the south. Popular with over-60s who have been on the island for many years and have moved away from the resort model. More Spanish is required in daily life, but the cultural richness is considerable.

A note on interior towns: they are beautiful, but services, English-speaking community infrastructure, and healthcare access are all more limited. Unless you are specifically seeking that kind of immersive, rural Spanish life, the resort south or Puerto de la Cruz will serve you better as a retirement base.

Healthcare for Retirees in Tenerife

Healthcare deserves its own careful attention, because it is the most significant practical concern for most retirees — and also one of Tenerife's genuine strengths.

Private health insurance is required for the Non-Lucrative Visa. This is a visa condition, not optional, and the policy must have no exclusions and no co-payments for routine care. The good news is that private healthcare in Tenerife is both high quality and genuinely affordable.

The major private hospital chains in the south — Hospiten and Vithas — operate with English-speaking staff throughout. Standards are good. Specialists, physiotherapists, and dentists are widely available in the resort areas. Hospital Universitario de Canarias in La Laguna is the main public hospital for complex or serious cases and serves both north and south.

For a healthy 65-year-old, a comprehensive private health policy typically costs approximately €150–€250 per month. This rises with age and pre-existing conditions, but most retirees find it significantly cheaper than equivalent private cover at home — and the standard of care is comparable.

British retirees — the S1 form: This is worth understanding before you arrive. British retirees who receive a UK State Pension can apply for an S1 healthcare export certificate from the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP). The S1 allows you to register with the Spanish public health system (via the INSS) and receive healthcare funded by the UK. This can significantly reduce or eliminate the need for private insurance costs once you are registered in Spain. The process has administrative steps and nuances — we recommend discussing it with a specialist before your application, as it can make a meaningful difference to your monthly budget.

After five years of continuous legal residence and becoming a tax resident, broader access to the Spanish public system becomes available. After ten years, Spanish citizenship is potentially achievable depending on nationality.

The Non-Lucrative Visa for Tenerife — What You Need

The Non-Lucrative Visa is the standard route for non-EU retirees — British, American, Canadian, Australian, South African, and others — who wish to live in Spain without taking employment. It is designed precisely for people living on pensions, investment income, or savings.

Key requirements for 2026:

  • Proof of passive income of approximately €2,400/month for a single applicant (linked to the Spanish IPREM rate — verify the current figure, as it is reviewed annually)
  • Around €600/month additional income required per dependent partner or family member
  • Valid passport (with sufficient remaining validity)
  • Criminal record certificate from each country of residence over the past five years
  • Medical certificate confirming no infectious diseases of public health concern
  • Proof of health insurance with no exclusions and no co-payments, valid across Spain
  • Proof of accommodation in Spain (rental contract or property deed)
  • Sufficient funds evidence (bank statements, pension letters, investment statements)

The visa is applied for at your Spanish consulate before you move — you cannot apply from within Spain. It is initially granted for one year, then renewable for two-year periods. After five years of continuous legal residence, you become eligible for long-term EU residency status. After ten years, Spanish citizenship may be available depending on your nationality.

The application process is document-heavy and precise. A single error — wrong apostille, missing translation, incorrect certification — can result in refusal. Working with experienced specialists significantly improves your chances of a clean, first-time approval.

UK Pension Considerations and the S1 Form

For British retirees, several pension and financial considerations are worth understanding before you make the move.

The UK State Pension is paid in full regardless of where you retire — there is no reduction for moving to Spain. It can be paid directly to a Spanish bank account, and many people prefer this for simplicity. Personal and workplace pensions can similarly be paid to a Spanish account, though you should confirm arrangements with your pension provider before you move.

The S1 form (issued by HMRC/DWP to UK State Pension recipients) allows you to access Spanish public healthcare with the cost covered by the UK. Given that private insurance is required for the visa, the S1 does not eliminate that requirement at the point of application — but once you are registered with the INSS in Spain using the S1, your healthcare costs can drop significantly. This is worth thousands of pounds over a retirement. A specialist who understands both UK and Spanish systems can help you navigate the timing and process correctly.

Tax is an area where specialist advice is non-negotiable. After 183 days in any calendar year in Spain, you become a Spanish tax resident. This affects how your UK pension income, investment returns, and any UK property rental income is treated — the Spain-UK double taxation treaty applies, but the interaction is not always straightforward. We strongly recommend taking professional tax advice from a cross-border specialist before and shortly after arriving.

Currency is a practical consideration for those drawing income in sterling. EUR/GBP fluctuations affect your purchasing power month to month. Most British retirees use currency exchange services or a multi-currency account to manage this — transferring larger amounts at favourable rates rather than letting a bank convert monthly at unfavourable rates.

Community and Social Life

This is genuinely one of Tenerife's strongest cards for retirees — and it is not always covered adequately in practical guides that focus primarily on costs and visas.

The English-speaking expat community in the south of Tenerife is vast, well-organised, and long-established. This is not a fragile, recently arrived community of digital nomads — it is a decades-deep network of people who have built lives here and created genuine social infrastructure.

Organised clubs exist for golf, bowls, walking, bridge, swimming, petanque, and amateur dramatics. English-language churches hold regular services. Charity networks run fundraising events, coffee mornings, and community outreach. British and Irish newspapers are available; English-language radio stations broadcast locally. Organised excursions to other parts of the island are run regularly for those who want to explore without driving.

Many retirees who arrived in Tenerife with some anxiety about whether they would find a social life report that their calendar here is more active than anything they experienced at home. The combination of a willing community, good weather that enables outdoor socialising year-round, and the shared experience of being expats in a new country creates a social intensity that surprises many people.

For retirees who want Spanish integration rather than an English-speaking bubble: Spanish classes are widely available throughout the island, and Puerto de la Cruz in the north offers a much more organic Spanish social life for those willing to make the linguistic investment. The north's expat community is smaller and less visible — but those who choose it often describe it as more genuinely immersive.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much income do I need to retire in Tenerife on the NLV?
The Non-Lucrative Visa requires you to demonstrate approximately €2,400 per month in passive income — pension, investments, or savings — for a single applicant. This is roughly €28,800 per year. The exact figure is linked to the Spanish IPREM rate and reviewed periodically. Additional income of around €600 per month is required per dependent partner or family member.
Is private healthcare in Tenerife expensive?
No — it is one of the most affordable aspects of retiring in Tenerife. A comprehensive private health policy for a healthy 65-year-old typically costs €150–€250 per month. Hospital groups like Hospiten and Vithas operate across the south of the island with English-speaking staff. Private healthcare is required as a visa condition for NLV holders, but most retirees find the quality and value genuinely impressive compared to home.
Can British retirees use the S1 form in Tenerife?
Yes, in principle. British retirees who receive a UK State Pension can apply for an S1 healthcare export certificate from the DWP. This can allow them to register with the Spanish public health system and receive care funded by the UK, reducing or eliminating the need for private insurance. The process requires INSS registration in Spain. We recommend discussing this with a specialist, as the rules and practicalities have nuances.
What is the social life like for retirees in Tenerife?
Very active, particularly in the south. The English-speaking expat community in the Costa Adeje and Los Cristianos area is one of the largest in Spain. Organised clubs cover golf, bowls, walking, bridge, swimming, amateur dramatics, and more. English-language churches, charity networks, and organised excursions are well-established. Many retirees describe their social calendar in Tenerife as fuller than it was at home.
Do I need to speak Spanish to retire in Tenerife?
Not in the south resort areas — English is so widely spoken that daily life is entirely manageable without Spanish. That said, some Spanish makes interactions smoother, opens doors to deeper community integration, and is required for formal administrative processes. Puerto de la Cruz and the north require more Spanish for comfortable daily living.
Which part of Tenerife is better for retirees — north or south?
The south — Costa Adeje and Los Cristianos — is the established choice for most retirees, offering guaranteed sunshine, the most developed English-speaking infrastructure, and the largest expat social scene. Puerto de la Cruz in the north suits retirees who want a more authentically Spanish environment at lower cost, but with a cooler, occasionally cloudier climate.

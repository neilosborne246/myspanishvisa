Moving to Mallorca: The Expat's Complete Guide for 2026

Turquoise water, mountain villages, a proper capital city, and one of Europe's most established expat communities. Here's what you actually need to know before you move to Mallorca.

Home / Blog / Moving to Mallorca: The Expat's Complete Guide for 2026

Mallorca is the largest of Spain's Balearic Islands and, for many English-speaking expats, a serious rival to the mainland as a place to build a life. The combination of a Mediterranean climate, a functioning international airport with direct flights to most European cities, a vibrant capital in Palma, and some of the most beautiful coastline in Europe makes it a compelling choice.

It is not, however, a simple paradise with no trade-offs. The tourism economy shapes daily life in ways that visitors never see. Property is expensive and supply is limited. And the island's rhythm changes dramatically between summer and the quieter months. Understanding all of this before you move saves considerable disappointment later.

Is Moving to Mallorca a Good Idea?

For the right person, absolutely. Mallorca suits people who want access to outstanding natural beauty — mountains, beaches, and a diverse interior — combined with the infrastructure of a well-connected European destination. The island welcomes around 20 million visitors through Palma airport annually, which means the logistics of international travel are genuinely easy.

The expat community is large and well-established, with significant British, German, and Scandinavian populations. English is spoken widely in Palma and tourist-adjacent areas. Private international schools serve families with children. The public hospital, Son Espases in Palma, is one of the largest and most modern in Spain.

What Mallorca is not: cheap. It is not a place you move to in order to stretch a modest income. Property prices and rents have risen sharply, and the island's tourism economy means that some goods and services cost more than on the mainland. This is the trade-off for living somewhere people genuinely want to visit.

The Seasonal Dimension

This is the aspect of Mallorca life that trips up the most potential movers. The island receives the majority of its annual visitors between May and September. During these months, coastal resort areas become extremely busy — roads clog, restaurants are full weeks in advance, and the social character of the island shifts to serve millions of temporary visitors.

From October to April, the mood changes entirely. Resorts close down. Some restaurants and shops operate limited hours or shutter completely. The mountains are walkable, the beaches empty, and Palma returns to something closer to a genuine city rather than a tourist hub. Many long-term expats describe these quieter months as their favourite time of year on the island.

The lesson: where you choose to live on Mallorca matters enormously. Palma, the capital, functions as a real city year-round. Inland villages like Sóller, Pollença, or Sineu retain their character throughout. Coastal resort zones are a different proposition entirely — stunning in summer, deserted or closed in winter.

Property and Rental Costs in Mallorca

Mallorca's property market is among the most expensive in Spain. Demand from Northern European buyers — particularly German and British — has pushed prices in desirable areas to levels that rival prime London suburbs. Here is an honest picture for 2026:

Rental costs

  • One-bedroom apartment in Palma city centre: €1,000–€1,500 per month
  • One-bedroom apartment outside Palma centre: €750–€1,100 per month
  • Two-bedroom apartment in Palma: €1,300–€2,000 per month
  • Rural inland village (one-bedroom): €600–€900 per month
  • Coastal property in peak areas (Port de Pollença, Deia area): from €1,500–€3,000+ per month

Purchase prices

  • Average price per square metre in Palma: €3,500–€5,500
  • Premium coastal and mountain areas: €5,000–€10,000+ per square metre
  • Inland villages: typically €2,000–€3,500 per square metre

Foreign buyers face no legal restrictions on purchasing property in Spain, but will need a Spanish NIE number (the foreigner identification number) before completing any purchase. Factor in purchase costs of around 10–12% above the purchase price for taxes, notary fees, and registry costs.

Where to Live in Mallorca

Palma

The capital is the natural first choice for most expats. It has everything a modern European city offers: supermarkets, hospitals, international schools, a vibrant restaurant scene, museums, and a busy cultural calendar. The old town is genuinely beautiful, and the seafront promenade is one of the finest in the Mediterranean. Palma is year-round, functional, and well-connected.

Sóller and the Serra de Tramuntana

Nestled in the mountains of the northwest, Sóller and its port attract expats who want beauty and community without urban noise. The area is more expensive than average, but the quality of life — orange groves, hiking trails, a charming town square — is hard to argue with. It suits couples and retirees particularly well.

Pollença and the North

The northern towns of Pollença and nearby Alcúdia are popular with British and German expats. Pollença has a strong international arts community, a weekly market, and excellent restaurants. Port de Pollença draws families during summer and retains a quieter community year-round. This part of the island fills up in peak season but remains liveable through the year.

Inland Villages

Sineu, Artà, Felanitx, and Petra are examples of interior market towns that offer authentic Mallorcan life at a fraction of coastal prices. They suit expats who want genuine immersion, are happy to drive, and don't require constant access to international amenities. These communities tend to have smaller expat networks and more Spanish-speaking social environments.

Visas and Residency for Non-EU Movers

Since Brexit, British nationals are in the same position as American, Canadian, Australian, and South African citizens when it comes to moving to Mallorca: they need a Spanish visa to live here legally for more than 90 days in any 180-day period.

The two most relevant options for most expats are:

Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV)

The NLV is for people who want to live in Spain without working — retirees, those living on investments, rental income, or pension income. It requires proof of passive income (approximately €2,400 per month for a single applicant in 2026), private health insurance, a clean criminal record, and a medical certificate. It is applied for at your Spanish consulate before you move. Initial validity is one year, renewable for two-year periods.

Digital Nomad Visa (DNV)

The DNV is designed for remote workers employed by, or freelancing for, companies based outside Spain. It allows you to live in Spain and work legally for your foreign clients or employer. Income requirements are lower than the NLV (around 200% of the Spanish minimum wage), and it is particularly suited to younger expats who want to enjoy island life while continuing their careers.

Both applications require careful document preparation. Working with immigration specialists avoids the common errors that lead to delays or refusals.

Building a Life and Social Network in Mallorca

One of Mallorca's genuine strengths for incoming expats is the ready-made international community. The island has well-established English-speaking social groups, sports clubs, sailing associations, hiking clubs, and cultural societies. Finding your people is considerably easier than in many less-visited corners of Spain.

Learning Spanish — or even some Mallorcan, the local variant of Catalan — significantly enriches your experience and your relationships with long-term residents. English gets you far in Palma and tourist areas, but makes you dependent on the expat bubble in ways that many residents come to find limiting after a few years.

Frequently Asked Questions

What visa do British or American citizens need to move to Mallorca?
Non-EU citizens — including British nationals post-Brexit, Americans, Canadians, Australians, and South Africans — need a Spanish residency visa to live in Mallorca long-term. The Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) is the most common route for retirees and those living on passive income. Remote workers and freelancers typically apply for the Digital Nomad Visa instead.
How much does it cost to live in Mallorca?
A comfortable single-person lifestyle in Mallorca costs roughly €1,800–€2,600 per month, depending on location and habits. Palma city centre is the most expensive area. Renting a one-bedroom apartment in Palma costs approximately €1,000–€1,400 per month; outside the city or in inland towns, you can find options from €700–€900.
Is Mallorca too touristy to live in year-round?
It depends heavily on where you live. Palma functions as a genuine year-round city with relatively stable daily life. Coastal resort areas become very busy from May to September and significantly quieter the rest of the year. Many expats choose to live in or near Palma, or in inland villages, to avoid the seasonal extremes of the resort zones.
Can I buy property in Mallorca as a foreigner?
Yes. There are no restrictions on foreign nationals buying property in Spain, including Mallorca. However, you will need a Spanish NIE number before you can complete a purchase. Property prices in Mallorca are high by Spanish standards — average prices in sought-after areas range from €3,500 to over €8,000 per square metre.
What is the healthcare situation in Mallorca?
Mallorca has good public hospital facilities centred on Son Espases in Palma, one of the largest hospitals in Spain. Non-EU residents on an NLV are required to hold private health insurance; private clinics in Palma are well-equipped and often have English-speaking staff due to the large international population.
How do I get around Mallorca without a car?
Within Palma, the EMT bus network and a growing cycling infrastructure make car-free life manageable. Between Palma and other towns, there are train services to Inca and Manacor, and bus connections to most other areas. However, if you plan to live in a rural village or want easy access to less-connected spots, a car is a significant practical advantage.

Living in Fuerteventura as an Expat: Beaches, Wind, and Wide Open Space

Fuerteventura is not Tenerife. It is wilder, rawer, and considerably less manicured. It has Europe's finest Atlantic beaches, the wind that makes it one of the world's great surf and kite destinations, and a landscape that makes you feel the scale of the ocean. For expats who want something that has not been polished smooth, it is unlike anywhere else.

The second largest of the Canary Islands sits 100 kilometres off the north-west coast of Africa, shaped by ancient volcanic forces into something that looks, on approach, almost Saharan: pale sandy plains, low bare mountains in shades of ochre and rust, and then suddenly — as you cross a ridge or round a headland — a beach of such length and whiteness that you stop and stare.

Fuerteventura has more beach than any other Canary Island. Its coastline is a succession of long, wide, sandy beaches that seem to continue beyond where the eye can follow. The most famous are the Corralejo dunes in the north — a protected natural park where white sand dunes ripple against a backdrop of volcanic mountains — and the beaches of the Jandía peninsula in the south, where miles of pale sand face a swell that has crossed the entire Atlantic.

The wind comes with the territory. Fuerteventura is consistently one of the windiest inhabited places in Europe, which is exactly why it is rated as one of the world's premier windsurfing and kitesurfing destinations. That same wind shapes daily life in ways that matter when you are considering living here long-term.

What Fuerteventura Is (and Is Not)

Fuerteventura has not had its Manrique. There is no island-wide aesthetic code governing building colours or heights the way Lanzarote does. Parts of it are scruffier, more improvised, less designed. The capital, Puerto del Rosario, is functional but not particularly attractive. And that is fine — because the island's character comes from its landscape and its openness, not from its architecture.

If Lanzarote is an island that has been carefully curated, Fuerteventura is an island that has been left more or less to be itself. There is more space between settlements, more silence between the waves, more sky. People who love Fuerteventura tend to love it with some intensity. It produces a particular kind of attachment — to its light, its scale, its Atlantic rawness.

The expat community here is smaller and quieter than in Lanzarote, less dominated by the kind of British-resort infrastructure you find in Puerto del Carmen. There are established communities of British and German long-term residents, particularly in Corralejo and Caleta de Fuste — but the atmosphere is more integrated, less bubble-like.

The Areas: Where Expats Live

Corralejo (North)

Corralejo is the island's most lively town for expats and the natural focus for anyone wanting community, restaurants, bars, and proximity to the famous dunes. It has a genuinely international character — British, German, Dutch, Scandinavian, Spanish — without the full resort-strip atmosphere of some tourist towns. The beachside strip and old town area have a decent selection of restaurants and bars. There is a functioning social scene.

The surf and windsurf culture is strong here — schools, hire shops, and the community that forms around water sports create a more active, younger energy than elsewhere on the island. The ferry to Lanzarote (Playa Blanca) runs daily from Corralejo, making inter-island travel easy.

Long-term residents tend to live in the residential areas away from the main tourist strip, where rents are lower and the pace is slower. As in all resort towns, the tourist season brings noise and crowds; the quieter months (November–February) reveal a different, more local character.

Caleta de Fuste (Mid-island)

Caleta de Fuste is a planned resort development about 10 kilometres south of the capital — it has a horseshoe bay, a good beach, restaurants, and a notably calm atmosphere. It is more family and retirement-oriented than Corralejo, better organised, and quieter. The expat community here tends to be older and more settled.

Its proximity to the airport (10 minutes) is either a plus or a minus depending on your perspective. The flight paths are sometimes audible. The trade-off is genuine convenience for those who travel regularly.

Costa Calma and Jandía (South)

The south of the island is the domain of the beaches — particularly the extraordinary long beaches of the Jandía natural park. Costa Calma is a resort strip oriented around these beaches, with a significant German expat and tourist presence. It is quiet, relatively isolated, and beautiful. Suited to those who want beach living above all else and do not mind the distance from the island's other centres. Services are limited compared to Corralejo; a car is even more essential here than elsewhere.

The Wind: What You Need to Know

This deserves its own section because it is genuinely important. The Alisio trade winds blow from the north-east across Fuerteventura almost year-round, and they are not gentle. They are persistent, sometimes strong, and they shape daily life on the island in ways that are not visible from holiday brochures.

What the wind means in practice:

  • The north of the island (Corralejo) is consistently windier than the south (Jandía), which is partly sheltered by the mountains
  • Outdoor café terraces and beach days require windbreak awareness — eating outdoors in summer in Corralejo can be a sandy, hair-in-face affair
  • Windsurfing and kitesurfing are genuinely world-class and freely accessible
  • Winter winds can be cold by Canary Islands standards, even when the sun is out
  • Some people find the constant movement of air energising; others find it relentless

Spend time here in October or November before making a long-term commitment. The autumn is when the trade winds are at their strongest. If the wind bothers you after a week, it will bother you after a year.

Cost of Living in Fuerteventura

Fuerteventura is one of the most affordable Canary Islands options — slightly cheaper than Lanzarote in some areas, and significantly cheaper than mainland Spain or northern Europe.

Rent

  • One-bedroom apartment, Corralejo residential area: €600–€800 per month
  • One-bedroom apartment, Caleta de Fuste: €580–€780 per month
  • Two-bedroom apartment with sea views, Corralejo: €800–€1,100 per month
  • Costa Calma and Jandía: generally 10–20% lower than Corralejo

Monthly budget for a single person

  • Rent (one-bedroom): €600–€800
  • Groceries: €200–€270
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet): €100–€140
  • Private health insurance (varies by age): €80–€180
  • Car costs (insurance, fuel, maintenance): €150–€250
  • Dining out, leisure, water sports activities: €200–€400
  • Total: approximately €1,350–€2,050 per month

The Canary Islands' IGIC tax at 7% (compared to mainland Spain's 21% VAT) keeps grocery, restaurant, and service costs noticeably lower. Property purchase prices are also accessible — a two-bedroom apartment in Corralejo typically sells for €170,000–€260,000.

Getting Around

You need a car. The island is roughly 100 kilometres from north to south and buses connect the major towns, but services are infrequent and the schedule is not designed around expat daily life. A car opens up the island — its remote beaches, its inland mountain roads, the quiet southern peninsula. Most expats acquire a car early.

Fuel in the Canary Islands is cheaper than in mainland Europe due to lower fuel taxes. Insurance costs less than in the UK. Running a car in Fuerteventura is significantly more affordable than in northern Europe.

Healthcare

The main public hospital is the Hospital General de Fuerteventura in Puerto del Rosario. It covers the island's emergency and general medical needs adequately. Private healthcare options are more limited than on Tenerife or Gran Canaria — there are private clinics in Corralejo and Caleta de Fuste, but specialist provision is restricted.

This is worth being honest about. If you have complex, ongoing medical needs that require regular specialist consultations, Fuerteventura's healthcare infrastructure is a genuine consideration. Serious or complex cases may require transfer to Gran Canaria. For most routine healthcare needs — GP visits, physiotherapy, dental care, minor procedures — the combination of private clinics and the public hospital covers the base adequately.

Non-Lucrative Visa and Digital Nomad Visa holders must hold private health insurance as a visa condition.

Expat Community

The expat community in Fuerteventura is smaller and quieter than in Lanzarote's Puerto del Carmen, but it is genuinely established — particularly in Corralejo and Caleta de Fuste. There are English-speaking estate agents, doctors, legal advisers, and the kind of informal social networks that make practical life easier when you are new to a place.

The German expat community is notably large here — Fuerteventura has been popular with German residents since the 1980s, and German is as commonly heard in some areas as English. This multinational character gives Fuerteventura a different feel from the predominantly British resort culture of southern Tenerife or Puerto del Carmen in Lanzarote.

Visas for Living in Fuerteventura

Non-EU citizens need a Spanish residence visa to live in Fuerteventura long-term. The main routes:

  • Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV): For retirees and those with passive income. Requires proof of approximately €2,400 per month for a single applicant and private health insurance with no co-payments. The correct route for most British retirees.
  • Digital Nomad Visa (DNV): For remote workers employed outside Spain or running location-independent businesses. Requires proof of remote income above approximately €2,300 per month. Valid throughout Spain including the Canary Islands.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Fuerteventura a good place to live as an expat?
Yes, for the right person. Fuerteventura suits expats who want space, extraordinary beaches, an outdoor lifestyle, and lower costs than most European destinations. It has an established British and German expat community in Corralejo and Caleta de Fuste. The island is rawer and less polished than Tenerife or Gran Canaria — that is part of its appeal. If you want a buzzing city or heavy expat infrastructure, look elsewhere. If you want wide open Atlantic landscape and one of Europe's best surf coasts, Fuerteventura is genuinely compelling.
How much does it cost to live in Fuerteventura?
Fuerteventura is one of the most affordable Canary Islands options. A one-bedroom apartment rents for €550–€800 per month. A comfortable single-person life including rent, groceries, utilities, a car, and leisure typically costs €1,350–€2,000 per month. The Canary Islands' IGIC at 7% (vs mainland 21% VAT) keeps everyday costs noticeably lower.
What is the best area to live in Fuerteventura as an expat?
Corralejo in the north is the most vibrant — surf culture, international restaurants, lively waterfront. Caleta de Fuste (mid-island) is calmer, more family-oriented, and well-organised. Costa Calma and Jandía in the south offer spectacular beach isolation. Most long-term expats choose Corralejo or Caleta de Fuste for the balance of community and access.
How windy is Fuerteventura to live in?
Very. Fuerteventura is consistently one of the windiest inhabited places in Europe. The Alisio trade winds blow from the north-east almost year-round — stronger in the north than the south. For surfers and outdoor enthusiasts this is a feature; for those who find persistent wind tiring, it is a significant consideration. Visit in October or November before committing.
Do I need a car to live in Fuerteventura?
Yes. Public transport is limited and infrequent. The island is roughly 100km north to south and a car is essential for daily life and exploring the beaches. Running costs are reasonable — fuel and insurance are cheaper than in mainland Europe due to lower Canary Islands taxes.
What is healthcare like in Fuerteventura?
The Hospital General de Fuerteventura in Puerto del Rosario covers general medical needs. Private clinics in Corralejo and Caleta de Fuste serve the expat population for routine care. Specialist provision is limited compared to the larger Canary Islands — complex cases may require transfer to Gran Canaria. For most routine healthcare needs the provision is adequate, but this is worth weighing honestly if complex medical care is a regular requirement.

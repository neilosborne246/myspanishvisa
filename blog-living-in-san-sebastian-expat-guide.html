Living in San Sebastián as an Expat: The Basque Country's Secret

Imagine a city of 187,000 people with more Michelin stars per capita than anywhere else in Europe, a horseshoe bay of such perfection that architects still argue it could not have been planned, and an old quarter so dense with extraordinary bars that choosing where to eat becomes the central pleasure of each day. San Sebastián is not what most people picture when they think of Spain. That is precisely the point.

Walk into the Parte Vieja on any Thursday evening and you will encounter something unlike anything else in Europe. The old quarter of Donostia–San Sebastián — a dense grid of medieval streets a few blocks from the waterfront — contains one of the greatest concentrations of bars per square metre on the continent. But these are not ordinary bars. Each one offers a counter loaded with pintxos: small, precisely constructed bites of food that in their best versions approach the complexity of restaurant cooking. Seared foie on brioche with a reduction. Bacalao croquettes so light they seem improbable. Grilled prawn skewers on a slice of bread that has been absorbing the juices of a thousand previous prawns. You take what you want, keep your cocktail stick as a tally, and move to the next bar. An evening in the Parte Vieja — moving through six or eight bars, eating twelve to fifteen pintxos, drinking txakoli or red wine or a caña — costs somewhere between €15 and €25 per person and constitutes one of the finest eating experiences in Europe.

This is not accidental. The Basque Country has a culinary culture that is decades deep and institutionally serious. San Sebastián has produced three-Michelin-star restaurants including Arzak and Mugaritz that rank among the best in the world. The Basque Culinary Center, a university dedicated entirely to gastronomy, is based here. The chefs who graduate from it, and the culture that produces them, feed back into even the humblest neighbourhood bar. Food in San Sebastián is taken seriously at every level, from a pintxos counter at 7pm to a tasting menu that requires a booking three months in advance. For expats who care about food — and specifically those who want their everyday life to involve extraordinary eating without extraordinary expense — there is no better city in Europe.

What Makes San Sebastián Special

Food is the headline act, but it is not the only reason people move to San Sebastián and refuse to leave. The city is small — around 187,000 people in the city proper — and that compactness is itself a quality-of-life factor. Everything is walkable. La Concha, the horseshoe-shaped bay right in the city centre, is consistently rated among the most beautiful urban beaches in Europe. The sheltered, calm water and the surrounding promenade mean that on any fine afternoon the beach is genuinely usable, not just photogenic. Behind the city, the green Basque hills rise immediately. Monte Urgull overlooks the old quarter from one side; Monte Igueldo from the other. The scale of it — city, beach, hills, Atlantic — within perhaps three kilometres feels implausible.

San Sebastián is also one of Spain's safest cities. The streets of the Parte Vieja at 2am are busy, festive, and overwhelmingly peaceful. The sense of civic order that characterises the Basque Country generally is evident throughout the city: parks are maintained, public transport runs on time, streets are clean. For families moving from larger cities where safety is a practical daily consideration, this is not a minor point.

The Atlantic Climate — Not the Spain You Are Imagining

This is the section that many potential movers skip over, and they shouldn't. San Sebastián has an Atlantic climate. It is green. It rains — frequently, seriously, and throughout the year. The city averages around 170 or more days with measurable rainfall annually. In a typical year, there is no month in which you can reliably expect dry weather. The streets of the Parte Vieja shine with rain on an October Tuesday afternoon in a way that is genuinely beautiful. The hills behind the city stay green year-round in a way that is genuinely surprising for anyone arriving from southern Spain. But it is real rain, not decorative rain.

What the climate is not is cold. Winters in San Sebastián are mild by Atlantic standards. Temperatures from December through February typically range from 8 to 12°C. It gets grey. It can be relentlessly grey for days at a time. But hard frost is rare, and snow in the city itself is unusual. Summers are mild and genuinely pleasant — July averages around 18 to 22°C, evenings often require a light jacket, and the kind of suffocating heat that descends on Seville or Murcia in August is entirely absent. If you have spent August in Andalusia sweating through your sleep, a San Sebastián summer feels like a gift.

Expats from northern Europe — particularly from the UK, Ireland, the Netherlands, or Germany — adapt to this climate with remarkable ease. It is not unlike home, just milder, with better food. The surf community loves it: Zurriola beach, on the Gros side of the city, is a serious surf break that functions year-round. The hiking community loves it: the green Basque countryside is extraordinary and the rain is what makes it so. The people who struggle are those who moved to Spain specifically for the sun and find that the Basque Country has sent them a different weather system entirely. If sunshine is your primary motivation, San Sebastián is the wrong city.

Neighbourhoods — Where to Live in San Sebastián

San Sebastián is small enough that no neighbourhood is inconveniently located, but each has a distinct character that will suit different kinds of expat.

Parte Vieja

The old quarter is where you go for dinner, not necessarily where you live. The pintxos bars are the attraction and the noise — evenings here are busy and loud until late. Apartments exist but supply is constrained and prices high. Those who do live here are immersed in the social pulse of the city in a way that is exhilarating for some and exhausting for others.

Gros

Gros is the neighbourhood most commonly recommended for expats arriving in San Sebastián, and for good reason. It sits across the river Urumea from the old quarter, adjacent to Zurriola beach, and has developed its own food scene that in recent years has rivalled the Parte Vieja for pintxos quality. It is younger, slightly more affordable, and has the energy of a neighbourhood that knows it is the right place to be. Remote workers and younger professionals cluster here.

Centro

The commercial heart of the city, with the widest range of apartments, good transport connections, and a practical rather than atmospheric character. Centro suits those who want a functional base with everything available without the premium of the beachfront or old quarter.

Antiguo

A quiet, largely residential neighbourhood near the University of the Basque Country campus. Popular with academics and those attached to the university community. Calmer than Gros or the Parte Vieja, with good green space, and slightly removed from the tourist circuits that fill the city in summer.

Ondarreta

The western end of La Concha bay, beyond the Miramar Palace gardens, Ondarreta is upscale and quiet. The beach here, smaller than La Concha proper, is less crowded and the surrounding streets are lined with well-maintained apartment buildings and villas. This is where you live if you want beach proximity and peace, and you have the budget for it.

Cost of Living in San Sebastián

The honest answer is that San Sebastián is expensive by Spanish standards. It is not as expensive as central London or Zurich, but it sits closer to parts of Barcelona than to Seville, Valencia, or Alicante. Rental prices have risen significantly over the past decade and supply is constrained by the city's geography — mountains and sea on three sides leave limited room to expand.

Approximate monthly costs

  • 1-bedroom apartment in Gros or Centro: €1,000–€1,300 per month
  • 1-bedroom apartment near La Concha or in Ondarreta: €1,200–€1,500 per month
  • Groceries for one person: €250–€320 per month
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet): €100–€150 per month
  • Private health insurance: €80–€160 per month depending on age and coverage
  • Pintxos evening for two (six bars, ten to twelve pintxos each): €30–€50
  • Comfortable single-person monthly budget (including rent): approximately €2,000–€2,800

The food offset is real and significant. The Parte Vieja and Gros make it possible to eat at an extraordinarily high level for very modest money. An evening that would cost €120 at a mid-range restaurant in London or Dublin costs €20 in the pintxos bars. If you eat out frequently in the pintxos tradition rather than cooking at home, your food costs relative to quality are lower than almost anywhere in Europe. The rent, however, is the rent. There is no offset for that.

Basque Language and Culture

Euskara — the Basque language — is one of the great linguistic mysteries of Europe. It is unrelated to any other known language, predates the Indo-European languages that surround it, and is spoken by around 700,000 people, primarily in the Basque Country and Navarre on both sides of the French-Spanish border. In San Sebastián, Basque is co-official with Spanish and very much present: street signs are bilingual, bus announcements are in Basque, many businesses use Basque names, and a substantial portion of the city's population — particularly those educated after the post-Franco cultural revival — is fluent.

For practical daily life, none of this is a barrier. Spanish is fully sufficient for all administrative, commercial, and social purposes. Medical appointments, dealing with authorities, banking, shopping — all of it works in Spanish. English is increasingly spoken, particularly among younger Basques in hospitality, retail, and professional settings. Expats who speak no Basque whatsoever live perfectly comfortably here. What Euskara adds is cultural texture. The Basques have a fierce and justified pride in their language and their distinctiveness from the rest of Spain. Understanding something of that pride — and approaching the culture with curiosity rather than indifference — will open doors that would otherwise remain closed.

Basque culture extends beyond language. The traditional sports — pelota, rural strength competitions known as herri kirolak — are taken seriously. The gastronomic societies known as txokos, where members cook together and eat communally, are a Basque institution that has no equivalent elsewhere in Spain. The festivals are distinctive, particularly the July festivities of Semana Grande, when the city runs on concerts, events, and fireworks for nine days. If you are moving from a culture where civic and communal life feels thin, the richness of Basque cultural participation is a genuine revelation.

Outdoor Life and Nature

San Sebastián's geography means that outdoor life is built into the rhythm of the city rather than something you need to drive to. La Concha — the main bay — has a promenade that wraps around the water from the old quarter to Monte Igueldo, and walking it at any time of day or year is one of the city's defining pleasures. The beach itself is genuinely swimmable from June through September, and the sheltered bay makes it safe and calm in conditions that would churn a more exposed coastline.

Monte Urgull, the wooded hill above the Parte Vieja, has walking paths through pine and eucalyptus that bring you to a fortress and a statue of Christ above the town within fifteen minutes of leaving the pintxos bars. Zurriola beach, facing the open Atlantic rather than the sheltered bay, is a working surf break that attracts a serious surfing community year-round. Lessons and board hire are readily available for beginners.

Beyond the city, the Basque countryside is extraordinary. The Camino de Santiago — specifically the Camino del Norte — passes through San Sebastián, and the coastal stages between Donostia and Bilbao offer some of the finest walking on the entire route. The Pyrenees are within roughly two hours by road, opening up serious mountain walking and skiing in winter. The French Basque Country — Biarritz, Bayonne — is forty minutes away. San Sebastián sits at the meeting point of sea, mountain, and a national border in a way that makes it a natural base for a wide range of outdoor pursuits.

The Expat and International Community

San Sebastián's international community is smaller than those in Madrid, Barcelona, or the coastal tourist belt, and that is partly a function of the city's scale. At 187,000 people, it is a small city by any European measure, and its expat population reflects that. What the community lacks in size it compensates for in commitment: people who move to San Sebastián tend to stay. The quality of life is high enough, and the city sufficiently unusual, that the churn you see in more obviously expat-oriented cities is largely absent here.

The University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU) and the Basque Culinary Center bring an international academic and gastronomic community. IE Law School has a campus in the city. The technology sector, while modest compared to Madrid or Barcelona, has grown in recent years and draws younger international professionals. English-language social groups exist, and the Gros neighbourhood in particular has the kind of bar and cafe culture where it is easy to meet other foreigners without having to seek out organised expat events. But if you need a large, structured expat infrastructure — the kind you find in Malaga or Alicante — San Sebastián will feel thin on that front. It is a city where you integrate into Basque life rather than live alongside it.

Is San Sebastián Right for You?

San Sebastián is one of Europe's most remarkable small cities. The people who move here and thrive are those who value food as a daily pleasure rather than an occasional treat, who find the Atlantic landscape beautiful rather than grey, who want a compact and walkable city with genuine cultural depth, and who are willing to pay more for quality of life than they would in Murcia or Extremadura. It suits those from northern Europe who find the rest of Spain too hot, too flat, or too focused on beach tourism. It suits those who want sophistication and seriousness alongside outdoor access.

It is less obviously right for those on a tight budget, since the costs are real. It is not the city for those who need a large, well-established English-speaking community around them from day one. And if your vision of Spain involves terracotta rooftops under ferocious July sunshine, the Basque Country will be a particular kind of disappointment. The rooftops here are green with moss, and the July sun, when it comes, is gentle.

For those who can work within its realities, San Sebastián's reputation among people who know it is consistent and striking: those who move here rarely leave. That consensus is not accidental. It reflects a quality of daily life — the food, the safety, the scale, the nature — that is genuinely difficult to replicate elsewhere. If the profile fits, San Sebastián is a city that will exceed expectations in almost every direction.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is San Sebastián a good place to live as an expat?
Yes, for the right kind of expat. People who move to San Sebastián almost universally say they will never leave. The quality of life — food, safety, nature, culture — is extraordinary. The drawbacks are cost (higher than most of Spain) and the climate (genuinely rainy — around 170 or more wet days per year). If those do not deter you, San Sebastián is one of Europe's finest cities to call home.
How much does it cost to live in San Sebastián?
More than most Spanish cities. A single person living comfortably needs approximately €2,000–€2,800 per month, including rent (€1,000–€1,500 for a one-bedroom), food, utilities, and health insurance. The food offset is real though — the Parte Vieja's pintxos bars let you eat exceptionally well for €15–€25 per evening.
What is the weather like in San Sebastián?
Atlantic and green. Summers are mild (18–22°C average in July) and rarely hot — you will need a light jacket some evenings. Winters are mild but grey (8–12°C). Rain is frequent year-round — San Sebastián averages around 170 or more days with measurable rainfall annually. If you are from northern Europe or the UK, this is not unlike home, just milder. If you are expecting Andalusian sunshine, you are in the wrong city.
Do I need to speak Basque to live in San Sebastián?
No. Basque (Euskara) is everywhere on street signs, menus, and in Basque cultural life, but Spanish is fully co-official and sufficient for all practical daily needs. English is increasingly spoken, particularly among younger Basques. You will encounter Basque in shops, on buses, in administration — but never as a barrier. It adds cultural texture to life in the city rather than creating an obstacle.
Which neighbourhood is best for expats in San Sebastián?
Gros is popular with younger expats and professionals — it is near Zurriola surf beach, has a great food scene, and is more affordable than the Parte Vieja. Centro is practical and well-connected. Antiguo is quiet and close to the university, suited to those who prefer calm over buzz. Ondarreta and the area near La Concha suit those who want upscale quiet with beach proximity.
Is San Sebastián very expensive compared to other Spanish cities?
Yes, by Spanish standards. Rent in San Sebastián is comparable to parts of Barcelona — significantly higher than Valencia, Seville, or Alicante. However, it remains cheaper than London, Dublin, or Sydney. If you are coming from a major northern European or Anglophone city, San Sebastián's cost of living, while high for Spain, will likely feel manageable — and the quality of life return on that cost is remarkable.

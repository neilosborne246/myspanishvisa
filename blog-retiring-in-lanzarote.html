Retiring in Lanzarote: Warm Winters, Low Costs, and the Non-Lucrative Visa

Lanzarote has drawn British retirees for decades — and for good reason. A warm, dry climate, affordable costs, an established English-speaking community, and some of the most striking scenery in Europe. Here is the practical guide to making it your retirement home.

The British retirement community on Lanzarote is not new. People began arriving in the 1980s, drawn by cheap flights from the UK, temperatures that rarely dip below 15°C even in January, and living costs a fraction of what they faced at home. Many of those early arrivals are still here — they are grandparents now, settled deeply into island life, part of communities that have grown into something genuinely self-sustaining.

What has changed is the visa landscape. Since Brexit, British nationals can no longer simply arrive and stay. Like Americans, Australians, Canadians, and South Africans, you now need a Spanish residence permit. For retirees, that means the Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) — and it is a visa the island is well-accustomed to processing, because the demand has been there for years.

This guide covers everything you need to plan a Lanzarote retirement: where to live, what it costs, how the NLV works, and what the island actually offers over the long term.

Why Lanzarote for Retirement?

The short answer is climate, cost, and community — three things that matter disproportionately once you are no longer tied to a workplace.

The climate is Lanzarote's most consistent argument. Temperatures between 18°C and 26°C year-round, with virtually no rain and more than 300 days of sunshine. The winters are mild enough for outdoor dining in December. The summers are warm without the breathless heat of Andalusia in July. For people managing arthritis, respiratory conditions, or simply the desire to live outside, this matters in a way it cannot when you visit for a fortnight.

The cost argument is real. Living in Lanzarote on a UK pension is genuinely comfortable in a way that living in southern England — or even many other parts of Spain — increasingly is not. The Canary Islands' lower tax rate (IGIC at 7% rather than mainland VAT at 21%) feeds through to everyday shopping, restaurant bills, and property costs.

And the community is the thing that often seals it. For British retirees arriving without Spanish and without a ready-made social network, the existence of an established English-speaking community in Puerto del Carmen is not merely convenient — it is genuinely important for wellbeing. Integration into Spanish life is entirely possible if you choose it. But the scaffolding is there if you need it.

Best Areas for Retirement in Lanzarote

Puerto del Carmen — For Community and Convenience

Puerto del Carmen remains the dominant choice for British retirees, and the reason is straightforward: everything you need is here. The seafront promenade runs along several beaches. English-speaking doctors operate from established private clinics. There are English-language legal advisers, financial planners, and estate agents. There are social clubs, walking groups, book clubs, and bridge evenings. There are English supermarkets alongside Spanish ones.

It is a resort, which means it fills up with tourists in summer and empties somewhat in the quieter months. Long-term residents tend to prefer the residential streets behind the main tourist strip — quieter, more neighbourly, and cheaper. The Tías municipality that encompasses Puerto del Carmen extends inland to the town of Tías itself, where more Spanish residential life continues.

Playa Blanca — For Quiet and Quality

At the southern tip of the island, Playa Blanca is a different proposition — newer, quieter, and more composed than Puerto del Carmen. The beaches (Playa Dorada, and the spectacular natural beaches at Papagayo nearby) are genuinely beautiful. The marina area has good restaurants. The pace is slower.

The expat community here is smaller and less loud than in Puerto del Carmen. Suited to retirees who want a quieter life with easy access to Spain's best Atlantic beaches and less of the social bustle. The ferry to Corralejo on Fuerteventura departs from here — a pleasant day trip option.

Costa Teguise — For Balance

The northern resort strikes a balance between the full-on expat infrastructure of Puerto del Carmen and the solitude of Playa Blanca. A mix of Spanish families, Northern European expats, and a quieter pace. Good beaches, decent restaurants, and a cycling culture that many active retirees appreciate. Slightly more authentically Spanish in feel.

Monthly Cost of Living for Retirees

Budget-conscious retirement

  • Rent, one-bedroom apartment (inland or quieter area): €600–€750
  • Groceries: €200–€270
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet): €100–€140
  • Private health insurance (age 65–70): €100–€170
  • Transport (car costs, shared): €100–€150
  • Dining out, leisure, activities: €200–€350
  • Total: approximately €1,300–€1,850 per month

Comfortable retirement

  • Rent, one-bedroom or two-bedroom apartment (coastal area): €750–€950
  • Groceries and dining out: €450–€650
  • Utilities: €120–€160
  • Private health insurance: €130–€220
  • Transport (own car): €150–€250
  • Leisure, travel, cultural activities: €300–€500
  • Total: approximately €1,900–€2,750 per month

These figures are meaningfully lower than equivalent costs in the UK, Ireland, or most of northern Europe. A British couple drawing two UK State Pensions (£800–£900 per month each in 2026) can live comfortably in Lanzarote without drawing on savings — an increasingly rare feat in the UK itself.

The Non-Lucrative Visa: Your Route to Retirement

The Non-Lucrative Visa is the standard residence permit for non-EU retirees living in Spain without working. It is called "non-lucrative" because you are not permitted to carry out professional activity in Spain — you live on passive income.

Key requirements for 2026:

  • Passive income: Approximately €2,400 per month for a single applicant (around €28,800 per year). This can be a pension, investment income, rental income, or demonstrable savings. Couples require an additional circa €600 per month for the second applicant.
  • Private health insurance: A comprehensive policy with no co-payments, no exclusions for pre-existing conditions (ideally), valid throughout Spain. This is a hard requirement — not optional.
  • Clean criminal record: Background check from all countries you have lived in during the past five years, apostilled and often translated into Spanish.
  • Medical certificate: Confirming you do not carry diseases of public health concern. Issued by your own GP and typically apostilled.
  • Proof of accommodation: Either a rental contract or evidence of property ownership in Spain.

The NLV is initially granted for one year. It is then renewable for two-year periods — two years, then another two years, until you reach five years of legal residence, at which point long-term EU residence status becomes available. After ten years of continuous legal residence, Spanish citizenship is a possibility for some nationalities.

The application is submitted at your Spanish consulate (in the UK, this means London, Edinburgh, or Manchester, depending on your home address). The process typically takes four to eight weeks from submission to decision. Documentation requirements are precise and consulates differ in their interpretation — working with immigration specialists reduces the risk of avoidable refusal significantly.

Healthcare for Retirees in Lanzarote

Healthcare access depends on your specific situation:

NLV holders: private insurance required

All Non-Lucrative Visa holders must maintain private health insurance as a condition of their permit. This is not a bureaucratic formality — it is enforced at renewal. Policies must be comprehensive, with no significant exclusions and no co-payment structures that effectively make treatment unaffordable.

Private health insurance in Lanzarote is considerably cheaper than in the UK. A comprehensive policy for a 65-year-old typically costs €100–€180 per month, rising with age. Specialist private clinics in Puerto del Carmen and Arrecife handle most routine and moderate-complexity cases.

British retirees with an S1 form

British retirees receiving a UK State Pension are entitled to an S1 form from the NHS, which can be registered with the Spanish health system to access public healthcare without private insurance costs. This arrangement has continued post-Brexit for UK State Pension recipients. If you qualify, it is worth investigating before departure — it can represent a significant saving.

Public healthcare

The main public hospital, Hospital Doctor José Molina Orosa in Arrecife, handles the island's emergency and secondary care. It is a functioning, adequately equipped hospital. Serious cases — major trauma, complex surgery — may be transferred to a larger facility in Gran Canaria. There are also health centres in Puerto del Carmen and other areas for routine primary care.

Canary Islands Fiscal Advantages

Retiring in the Canary Islands is not merely an aesthetic choice — there are real financial advantages over mainland Spain:

  • IGIC at 7%: The Canarian indirect tax is 7% compared to mainland VAT at 21%. This applies to most goods and services.
  • Lower property transfer tax: IGTP at 6.5% on property purchases, compared to 8–10% on the mainland.
  • No Canary Islands wealth tax: Most residents are exempt from the annual Patrimonio (wealth tax) that applies in some mainland regions.
  • Lower cost base: The IGIC difference feeds through into the cost of groceries, restaurants, services, and building work in ways that compound significantly over a retirement.

Practical Considerations

Getting around

A car is essential. Public transport exists but is infrequent and not suited to daily retirement life. Most retirees bring or buy a car within their first months on the island. Running costs are reasonable — fuel and insurance are cheaper than in the UK.

Language

Spanish is the island's language. While English is widely spoken in the main tourist areas, making even a modest effort to learn Spanish enriches daily life considerably — particularly for dealing with Spanish institutions, healthcare, and the kind of genuine integration that transforms a place from a tourist location into a home.

The wind

Worth repeating: Lanzarote is windy. The trade winds are persistent and can be strong. This is not a dealbreaker for most people, but it is worth spending time here in autumn — not February — before committing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much income do I need to retire in Lanzarote on a Non-Lucrative Visa?
The Non-Lucrative Visa requires you to demonstrate passive income of approximately €2,400 per month for a single applicant (around €28,800 per year) for 2026. This can be from a pension, investments, savings, or rental income — it does not have to be employment income. Couples require an additional circa €600 per month for the dependent spouse.
What is the best area in Lanzarote for retirement?
Puerto del Carmen is the most popular choice for retirees who want a ready-made English-speaking community, beachside promenade living, and maximum convenience. Playa Blanca suits those who prefer a quieter, newer resort with excellent beaches and a calmer pace. Both areas have good English-language services and medical practices.
Is healthcare good enough in Lanzarote for retirees?
For routine and general healthcare, yes. The public hospital in Arrecife handles most medical needs, and private clinics in the main resort areas have English-speaking doctors. Serious or complex cases may require transfer to Gran Canaria or mainland Spain. NLV holders must maintain private health insurance as a visa condition. British retirees with a UK State Pension should investigate the S1 form for public healthcare access.
How much does it cost to retire in Lanzarote per month?
A comfortable retirement in Lanzarote typically costs €1,500–€2,200 per month for a single person, covering rent, groceries, utilities, private health insurance, and leisure. Couples can live very comfortably on €2,200–€3,000 combined — significantly cheaper than equivalent living in the UK or mainland European capitals.
Can British retirees use their S1 form in Lanzarote?
Yes. British retirees who receive a UK State Pension can register an S1 form with the Spanish health system, granting access to public healthcare. This applies in the Canary Islands as it does on the mainland. Arrange the S1 with the NHS before departure and register it with Spanish social security after arrival.
What are the Canary Islands fiscal advantages for retirees?
IGIC (local tax) is 7% compared to mainland VAT of 21%, which feeds through to lower everyday costs. Property transfer tax is 6.5% rather than mainland rates. There is no Canary Islands wealth tax for most residents. These advantages compound meaningfully over a retirement.

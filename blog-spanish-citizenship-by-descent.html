Spanish Citizenship by Descent: Who Qualifies and How to Claim It

Millions of people across Latin America, the UK, and beyond have Spanish blood in their family tree — and a legitimate legal right to reclaim the nationality that history took from their ancestors. Here is everything you need to know.

Home / Blog / Spanish Citizenship by Descent: Who Qualifies and How to Claim It

There is something significant happening in consulates across Buenos Aires, Mexico City, Havana, Montevideo, and Caracas. Every week, people clutching folders of old documents — birth certificates in faded ink, marriage records from towns they have never visited, photographs of grandparents who left and never came back — are lining up to claim something that was lost a long time ago. Spanish nationality. An EU passport. A connection to a country their family was forced to leave.

Spanish citizenship by descent is not a quirk of immigration law. It is the result of a specific history: a civil war, a dictatorship that lasted four decades, and the exile of hundreds of thousands of Spaniards who scattered across the Americas and beyond. Spain has been, slowly and imperfectly, trying to undo some of that. The Democratic Memory Law of 2022 is the most significant step yet.

This guide explains who qualifies, how the process works, what the documents look like, and what the realistic timeline is — without the vague promises or the template-language that surrounds this topic online.

The History Behind the Claim

To understand Spanish ancestry citizenship, you need to understand what happened. The Spanish Civil War (1936–1939) ended with Francisco Franco's forces victorious and a dictatorship that would last until 1975. In the years immediately surrounding the war — and in the decades that followed — perhaps 500,000 Spaniards went into exile. Many never returned. They settled in Mexico, Argentina, Cuba, the Dominican Republic, France, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom. They raised children there. Those children raised children of their own.

Many of the exiles lost their Spanish nationality — either explicitly stripped of it by the Franco regime, or effectively lost through decades of absence and the acquisition of foreign citizenship. Under Franco's Spain, renouncing or losing nationality was often not a choice but a consequence of political persecution, forced emigration, or simple survival.

Beyond the political exiles, there was also a wave of economic emigration: Spaniards who left during the lean decades of the 1940s, 50s, and 60s in search of work in Latin America and elsewhere. Their descendants may also have a claim, depending on the specific circumstances and the law that applies.

The Two Key Laws: What Changed and When

The Historical Memory Law (Ley de Nietos), 2007

Spain's first concrete legislative effort to address the diaspora of exiles came in 2007 with the Ley de Memoria Histórica — specifically, Article 20, which became known colloquially as the Ley de Nietos (the Law of the Grandchildren). For the first time, it created a pathway for grandchildren of Spaniards exiled during the Civil War and dictatorship to claim Spanish nationality. It had a fixed window: you had to apply between 2008 and 2011. When that window closed, tens of thousands of eligible descendants found themselves locked out.

The Democratic Memory Law, 2022

The Ley de Memoria Democrática (Democratic Memory Law), passed in October 2022, is the current operative legislation. It extended and improved on the 2007 law in several critical ways:

  • The application window is indefinite — there is no stated closing date
  • The eligible categories of descendants were broadened, including in some cases great-grandchildren
  • It covers not only political exiles but also those who emigrated for economic or work-related reasons during the Republic, the Civil War, or the dictatorship period
  • It removed some of the procedural obstacles that had frustrated applicants under the 2007 law

This is the law that currently governs Spanish citizenship by descent claims. Applications are open now.

Who Qualifies for Spanish Citizenship by Descent?

The eligibility criteria are specific, and it is worth being clear about them rather than giving false hope to people who do not genuinely qualify.

Category 1: Children and grandchildren of political exiles

If your parent or grandparent was a Spanish national who was exiled, fled, or was forced to emigrate due to political persecution — either during the Second Republic (1931–1936), the Civil War (1936–1939), or the Franco dictatorship (1939–1975) — you have a strong basis for a claim. This includes people who left to avoid imprisonment or execution, who were expelled, or who could not return because of their political affiliations or activities.

Category 2: Children and grandchildren of economic emigrants

Spanish nationals who emigrated for work or economic reasons during the covered period — and their descendants — may also qualify. The conditions are more nuanced here and depend on the specific circumstances of the emigration, so professional assessment of your family's case is important.

Category 3: Children of Spaniards (direct descent, always open)

Separately from the Democratic Memory Law, Spanish law has always allowed children of Spanish nationals to claim citizenship by descent (iure sanguinis). If one of your parents holds or held Spanish nationality, you can claim regardless of the historical context. This is not new law — it has always existed — and applies to people born to a Spanish parent anywhere in the world.

The Democratic Memory Law primarily addresses the harder cases: the grandchildren and great-grandchildren of people who lost their nationality, not the straightforward parent-child claims.

Important distinction: Spanish citizenship by descent is entirely separate from citizenship by naturalisation, which requires ten years of continuous legal residence in Spain for most nationalities (or two years for nationals of Ibero-American countries, the Philippines, Equatorial Guinea, Andorra, and Portugal). Citizenship by descent does not require you to have lived in Spain for a single day.

What Does a Spanish Passport Actually Give You?

This is worth stating plainly, because it is why so many people are pursuing these claims. A Spanish passport is an EU passport. It gives you — and your children — the unrestricted right to live, work, and study anywhere in the 27 member states of the European Union. That means Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal, Ireland, and 21 others. It means access to public healthcare systems, public universities at domestic tuition rates, and complete labour market freedom across the EU. For a family in Argentina, Mexico, or Cuba, that is not a bureaucratic technicality — it is a generational opportunity.

A Spanish passport also offers strong global mobility: visa-free or visa-on-arrival access to over 190 countries, including the United States, Canada, the UK, Japan, and Australia.

The Application Process: Step by Step

Step 1: Establish your eligibility

Before gathering a single document, you need to understand which category you fall under and whether your specific family history supports a claim. This involves knowing where your Spanish ancestor was born, what their nationality status was, when and why they left Spain, and how the line of descent runs from them to you. If there are gaps — an ancestor who was naturalised elsewhere before 1936, a generation where Spanish nationality may have lapsed — these need to be identified early.

Step 2: Determine your competent consulate

You apply through the Spanish consulate with jurisdiction over your place of habitual residence. This sounds simple but is a common source of errors. If you live in a country with multiple consulates (Argentina has several; Mexico has others), you must apply at the correct one. Submitting to the wrong consulate is a cause of delay and rejection.

Step 3: Gather and authenticate your documents

This is where most of the work sits, and where most errors occur. The document bundle must trace the line of descent from you back to your Spanish ancestor without gaps. Every certificate must be authenticated for use in Spain.

Step 4: Submit and wait

Once submitted, the consulate reviews the application and forwards it to the Civil Registry (Registro Civil) in Spain, which makes the final determination on nationality. Processing times are long. More on this below.

Step 5: Registration and passport

If the claim is approved, you are registered in the Spanish Civil Registry as a Spanish national. You can then apply for a Spanish passport and National Identity Document (DNI) through the consulate.

Documents You Will Need

The exact document requirements depend on your category and the generations involved, but the core bundle for a grandchild claim typically includes:

  • Your own birth certificate, apostilled (or legalised where apostille is not available) and with certified Spanish translation
  • Birth certificate of the parent through whom you claim
  • Birth certificate of the Spanish grandparent — this must be obtained from the Spanish Civil Registry in the town or province where they were born
  • Marriage certificates for every couple in the line of descent
  • Proof that the Spanish grandparent held Spanish nationality — a Spanish passport, a Spanish identity card, a certificate from the Spanish consulate register (padrón consular), or equivalent
  • Documentation proving the circumstances of exile or emigration — this could include political party membership records, exile organisation records, Spanish Republican refugee documentation, or emigration papers
  • Your current valid passport and, if applicable, nationality certificate from your country of birth

All foreign documents must carry an apostille stamp (for countries party to the Hague Convention) or go through full legalisation. Documents not in Spanish require certified translation by a sworn translator.

Obtaining the Spanish ancestor's birth certificate from the Civil Registry in Spain can take months on its own, particularly for records from small municipalities or from provinces where historical records were damaged during the Civil War. Starting this process early is not optional advice — it is essential.

Common documentation pitfalls: Gaps in the descent chain (a generation with no birth or marriage certificate), a Spanish ancestor who acquired foreign nationality before the covered period, certificates that are apostilled but not translated, or certificates obtained from unofficial sources — these are the most frequent causes of refusal. The standard required is high and the consulates have limited discretion to overlook missing evidence.

How Long Does It Take?

Honestly: longer than you would hope. The Spanish consulates in Latin American countries — particularly Buenos Aires, Mexico City, Havana, and Caracas — handle extremely high volumes of descent claims. Processing times of two to three years from submission to resolution are common. Some cases take longer. A small number move faster.

The Registro Civil Central in Madrid, which handles the nationality determination for many cases, also works through a substantial backlog. There is no expedited processing available for most applicants.

Delays are overwhelmingly caused by incomplete or deficient documentation. Applications that arrive complete and correctly authenticated move through more predictably than those that trigger queries or require the applicant to submit supplementary evidence mid-process.

This is the most important practical insight about the entire process: the time you invest before submission — in making sure every certificate is correct, every apostille is valid, every translation is sworn — directly reduces your total processing time. There is no shortcut, but there is preparation.

The Distinction That Matters Most: Descent vs. Naturalisation

People sometimes confuse Spanish citizenship by descent with the accelerated naturalisation route available to Ibero-Americans (Argentina, Mexico, Cuba, Colombia, and most other Spanish-speaking Latin American countries), which reduces the normal ten-year residency requirement to two years. These are different things.

Accelerated naturalisation through residency requires you to actually live in Spain — legally, continuously — for two years. You need a visa, a residence permit, tax residency, and you cannot leave for extended periods. You also need to pass a Spanish language test (DELE A2 or equivalent) and a constitutional knowledge exam (CCSE).

Spanish citizenship by descent requires none of that. You do not need to live in Spain. You do not need to speak Spanish to a tested standard. You do not need a visa or residence permit. If you qualify through ancestry, you can make the claim from your home country and never set foot in Spain until the day you collect your passport.

Both routes lead to Spanish nationality. The right route for you depends entirely on your family history and your circumstances.

What Latin American Applicants Should Know

The communities in Argentina, Uruguay, Chile, Mexico, Cuba, and Venezuela have the highest concentration of people with legitimate Spanish ancestry citizenship claims. This is not coincidence: these were the primary destinations for Spanish Republican exiles after 1939, and they also received large waves of economic emigrants during the Franco era.

In Argentina alone, it is estimated that several million people have Spanish grandparents or great-grandparents. The Spanish consulates in Buenos Aires, Córdoba, and Mendoza collectively receive thousands of descent applications each year. The volume is precisely why the processing times are so long — and why having a correctly prepared application matters so much. An incomplete file in that queue can sit for years waiting for a response to a query.

For Cuban applicants, there are additional complexities: obtaining documents from Cuban civil registries can be slow and unpredictable, and the Spanish consulate in Havana operates under particular constraints. Professional support in navigating these specific bureaucratic conditions is especially valuable.

For Venezuelan applicants, the political and practical situation adds further difficulty — obtaining authenticated documents from Venezuelan registries has become increasingly complicated. Early planning and professional guidance on sourcing documents is strongly recommended.

Getting Professional Help

A Spanish citizenship by descent claim is not like filling out a visa application form. It involves sourcing historical records from multiple countries, navigating the specific requirements of different consulates, obtaining documents from the Spanish Civil Registry (which itself requires knowing which municipality to contact), and assembling everything into a legally valid bundle.

Mistakes at the documentation stage — particularly submitting certificates that are incorrectly authenticated, sourced from unofficial registries, or presented without proper translation — are extremely difficult to recover from mid-process. The consulate does not guide you through corrections; it simply flags deficiencies and waits for you to resolve them, which adds months or years to your timeline.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who qualifies for Spanish citizenship by descent?
Under the Democratic Memory Law (2022), you may qualify if you are the child or grandchild of a Spanish national who lost or had to renounce their citizenship due to exile, political persecution, or emigration during the Second Republic, the Civil War (1936–1939), or the Franco dictatorship. Children of Spaniards who emigrated for economic reasons during the dictatorship period may also qualify under certain conditions. The connection must be provable through official documents tracing your family back to a Spanish-born ancestor.
What is the Ley de Nietos and is it still active?
The Ley de Nietos — formally part of the Historical Memory Law of 2007 — was the first legislation that opened a pathway for grandchildren of Republican exiles to claim Spanish nationality. Its application window closed in 2011. The 2022 Democratic Memory Law (Ley de Memoria Democrática) effectively reopened and extended this right on an indefinite basis, broadening the categories of eligible descendants. Applications under the Democratic Memory Law are currently open with no stated expiry date.
Do I need to speak Spanish to claim citizenship by descent?
No. Spanish citizenship by descent is not the same as citizenship by naturalisation. You do not need to pass a language test, a cultural knowledge exam, or demonstrate integration. The claim is based entirely on proving your family connection to a Spanish ancestor — the process is documentary, not assessed. That said, dealing with Spanish consulates and the Civil Registry can be easier with some Spanish, and professional assistance is strongly recommended.
What documents do I need to apply for Spanish citizenship by ancestry?
Core documents include: your birth certificate and those of every person in the descent line back to the Spanish ancestor; the Spanish ancestor's birth certificate (obtained from the Civil Registry in Spain); marriage certificates for every generation in the descent line; and documentation proving the Spanish ancestor held Spanish nationality. All foreign documents must carry an apostille and be accompanied by a certified Spanish translation. Documentation proving the circumstances of exile or emigration strengthens the case significantly.
Can I get a Spanish passport without living in Spain?
Yes. Spanish citizenship by descent does not require you to live in Spain, either before or after the claim is approved. The application is processed through your nearest Spanish consulate in your country of residence. Once nationality is granted and registered in the Civil Registry, you can apply for a Spanish passport from abroad. This is one of the most significant differences between citizenship by descent and citizenship by naturalisation — the latter requires years of actual residence on Spanish soil.
How long does Spanish citizenship by descent take to process?
Processing times vary significantly by consulate and the complexity of your documentation. In practice, applicants should expect between one and three years from submission to resolution. Consulates in Buenos Aires, Mexico City, Havana, and Caracas — which handle very high volumes of descent claims — frequently have the longest waits. Incomplete or deficient documentation is the most common cause of delays and rejections, which is why getting the document bundle right before submission matters enormously.

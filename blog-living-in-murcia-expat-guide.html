Living in Murcia as an Expat: Spain's Affordable, Overlooked Gem

Most people move to Spain and head for Malaga, Alicante, or the Canary Islands. Murcia, just south of the Valencia region on the southeast Mediterranean coast, keeps getting passed over. That is a mistake worth reconsidering.

Home / Blog / Living in Murcia as an Expat: Spain's Affordable, Overlooked Gem

Murcia does not appear in the headline conversations about where to move to Spain. It lacks the international profile of Barcelona, the coastal cachet of the Costa del Sol, or the island appeal of Tenerife and Gran Canaria. What it has instead is something rarer: low costs, a genuine Spanish city, a spectacular coastline that most British and American expats have never heard of, and local people who are warm towards foreigners in a completely unstudied way. The Region of Murcia has around 1.5 million people. It has a capital city of around half a million with a beautiful baroque cathedral and a lively university. It grows more than a third of Spain's fruit and vegetables. And it is, by almost any measure, one of the most affordable places in southern Europe to live well.

This guide makes an honest case for Murcia — including what it genuinely lacks. Less developed expat infrastructure than Malaga or Alicante. Less English spoken outside tourist and coastal zones. And summers that are among the most intense in continental Europe, with 40°C temperatures that are not a fluke but an annual reality. If you can work with those facts, Murcia might be the most underrated move you could make.

Two Very Different Murcas — The City and the Coast

Understanding Murcia means accepting that you are choosing between two quite distinct ways of life. They share a region, an identity, and a love of fresh produce and tapas. But the experience of living in Murcia city and living on the Costa Cálida are sufficiently different that they attract different kinds of expat.

Murcia city — the regional capital — is a proper Spanish city. Universities, professional infrastructure, a functioning cultural scene, Spanish professionals and families going about real Spanish life. It is not set up around expats. It does not cater to them the way Torrevieja or La Manga does. It rewards those who want genuine integration, who are comfortable building a life inside Spanish rhythms rather than alongside them.

The Costa Cálida — Murcia's Mediterranean coastline — is a different proposition. Here you find La Manga del Mar Menor, which has a long-established British community and the full range of English-speaking services that come with it. You find Mazarrón and Águilas, quieter and less developed, with more Spanish character. And you find the Mar Menor itself — the largest saltwater lagoon in Europe — which provides something genuinely unusual: calm, shallow, warm water that is ideal for elderly swimmers, families, and anyone who has been put off by the choppier Mediterranean elsewhere. The coast draws retirees. The city draws a more mixed crowd of younger expats, remote workers, and those who specifically want to avoid living in an expat bubble.

Murcia City — What to Expect Day to Day

Murcia city is a place that earns its affection rather than announces it. The old town is genuinely beautiful in the understated way of Spanish interior cities — the Catedral de Murcia, begun in the fourteenth century, dominates the centre and is worth a week of slow, repeated visits. The casino — a nineteenth-century private members' club with extraordinary interiors — is open to the public. The Glorieta de España, the main square, is a daily social hub in the way Spanish plazas are supposed to be.

The Universidad de Murcia and the nearby UCAM (Universidad Católica San Antonio) give the city a younger demographic than you might expect. Student culture means bars and restaurants are well-priced and abundant, opening hours are long, and there is a genuine evening energy that carries through the year. Murcia has a strong tapas tradition — in many bars, a tapa comes free with your drink, which is not universal in Spanish cities and says something about local generosity.

Day to day, Murcia city feels like a city that is proud of itself without being self-conscious about it. The Mercado de Verónicas, the central market, is the kind of market that reminds you why you moved to Spain in the first place: piles of locally grown fruit and vegetables, excellent fish from the nearby Mediterranean, butchers and cheese sellers who have occupied the same stalls for decades. The city centre is walkable. Public parks are well-maintained. The river Segura runs through the centre and the promenades on both banks are popular for evening walks.

What Murcia city does not have is a well-developed anglophone expat scene. There are international residents, and there is a growing number of younger foreign arrivals drawn by the cost and the quality of life. But if you are expecting the kind of social infrastructure you find in Alicante or Malaga — English-language social clubs, international schools on every corner, British supermarkets — Murcia city is not there yet. For some people that is an obstacle. For others it is exactly the point.

The Costa Cálida and La Manga

Murcia's coastline — the Costa Cálida, or "warm coast" — stretches for roughly 250 kilometres, with a character quite different from the more famous Costa Blanca to the north or the Costa del Sol to the west. It is less built-up in places, more genuinely Spanish in others, and home to one of the most distinctive geographical features in Mediterranean Spain.

The Mar Menor is a saltwater lagoon of approximately 170 square kilometres, separated from the open Mediterranean by a thin strip of land — La Manga del Mar Menor — running for about 22 kilometres. The lagoon water is calm, shallow (rarely deeper than seven metres), and several degrees warmer than the sea on the other side of the strip. It is the largest saltwater lagoon in Europe. For older residents, for those recovering from illness or injury, or simply for anyone who values safe, flat water over churning surf, the Mar Menor offers something genuinely uncommon on the Spanish coast.

La Manga del Mar Menor has been a popular British holiday destination since the 1970s, and that history has left a substantial permanent expat community. English is widely spoken. There are British-owned businesses, English-language services, and the comfortable social infrastructure that a long-established community builds over time. Property prices are relatively low by coastal Spanish standards. The pace of life is calm — La Manga functions more as a retirement and leisure destination than an active working community.

Beyond La Manga, the coastal towns of Mazarrón and Águilas deserve mention. Mazarrón — and particularly Puerto de Mazarrón — has a more mixed Spanish-and-foreign community, quieter than La Manga, with good beaches and lower property prices. Águilas, in the far south of the region, is largely Spanish in character and largely undiscovered by non-Spanish visitors. It has a very attractive old quarter, excellent fish restaurants, and a genuine local feel. For expats who want coastal life without the expat-bubble aspect, Águilas and Mazarrón are worth serious consideration.

Cost of Living in Murcia

Murcia consistently ranks as one of Spain's most affordable regions. The combination of low property costs, good local food production, and a lower-cost service economy means your money goes considerably further here than in Malaga, Valencia, or San Sebastián.

Murcia city

  • 1-bedroom apartment in the city centre: €450–€700 per month
  • 1-bedroom apartment outside the centre: €380–€550 per month
  • Groceries for one person: €180–€240 per month — lower than national average due to local production
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet): €90–€130 per month
  • Eating out: menú del día (three courses, drink included) €9–€12; tapas with drink €2–€4
  • Comfortable single-person monthly budget (including rent): approximately €1,200–€1,600

Costa Cálida and La Manga

  • 1-bedroom apartment in La Manga del Mar Menor: €500–€850 per month (seasonal variations apply — summer rates are higher)
  • 1-bedroom apartment in Mazarrón or Águilas: €400–€650 per month
  • Year-round coastal living costs slightly more due to tourism pricing in shops and restaurants close to the seafront
  • Comfortable single-person monthly budget on the coast: approximately €1,400–€1,800

Both figures compare very favourably with equivalent lifestyle costs in Alicante, Malaga, or anywhere on the Costa del Sol. Murcia is not just affordable for Spain — it is one of the most affordable regions in southern Europe for the quality of life it offers. A couple living on pension income can live very comfortably here without the financial pressure that comparable quality of life would impose in Barcelona or Madrid.

The Climate — What They Don't Tell You

Murcia's climate statistics read beautifully: more than 320 days of sunshine per year, mild winters typically ranging from 10 to 16°C, warm springs and golden autumns. This is all true, and the spring and autumn seasons in particular are genuinely exceptional — warm without being overwhelming, clear-skied, ideal for outdoor life.

What the promotional material tends to understate is summer. Murcia has one of the most intense summer climates of any major region in continental Europe. July and August in Murcia city routinely see daily temperatures of 40°C or above. Maxima of 43°C or 44°C are recorded most years. The heat is not a dry desert heat with cool evenings — Murcia can be humid, and nights do not always cool as dramatically as you might hope.

Spaniards in Murcia adapt to this the way they always do: activity shifts to the early morning and the evening, the afternoon hours are surrendered to shade and air conditioning, and the city develops a different rhythm during summer months. Air conditioning is not optional — it is essential infrastructure. Electricity bills in July and August will be noticeably higher than the rest of the year.

The coast has a genuine advantage in summer. The Mar Menor and the open Mediterranean coastline catch sea breezes that make temperatures feel more manageable. La Manga in August is significantly more comfortable than Murcia city in August, which is one reason the coastal areas fill up with both tourists and murcianos escaping the interior.

The honest assessment: Murcia's summers are a genuine deal-breaker for some people. If you find 30°C uncomfortable, you will find 40°C miserable. If you have medical conditions affected by extreme heat, take this seriously. For everyone else — for those who have summered in southern Spain and found it agreeable — Murcia's winters and shoulder seasons more than compensate. But go in with your eyes open about what July and August actually involve.

Fresh Food and Market Culture

The Region of Murcia is commonly described as Spain's orchard, and the description is apt. The region produces an extraordinary proportion of Spain's fruit and vegetables — tomatoes, peppers, lettuce, artichokes, lemons, apricots, peaches, melons — grown in the fertile land of the Segura river basin under near-constant sun. The practical consequence for anyone living here is that fresh produce is exceptionally good and exceptionally cheap.

Murcia city's Mercado de Verónicas is the heart of this food culture. An indoor market in a building dating from the 1920s, it offers the full range of locally grown produce alongside excellent local seafood — Murcia has a productive fishing fleet operating out of the Costa Cálida — alongside meat, charcutería, and cheeses. Shopping here, rather than in a supermarket, is not an affectation: it is genuinely the most economical and the most pleasurable way to eat in this city.

Murcia also has a strong tapas tradition. The old town in particular has a dense concentration of bars where the culture of the free tapa — a small plate of food accompanying each drink, at no extra charge — is deeply embedded. This is one of those local customs that makes daily life meaningfully more enjoyable and meaningfully cheaper than the posted price of a beer would suggest.

Community and Social Life

The social landscape in Murcia splits clearly along the city-coast divide. On the coast — and particularly in La Manga del Mar Menor — there is a well-established British community with the social infrastructure that implies: English-speaking groups, local organisations, informal social networks, and the general ease of finding people who share your language and background. For retirees particularly, this can matter a great deal in the early years of a move.

Murcia city is a different story. The expat community is smaller, younger, and less organised. It is growing — the combination of low costs and genuine urban quality of life is attracting increasing numbers of remote workers and younger Europeans — but it is not yet the kind of structured community you find in Alicante city or in Malaga. If you move to Murcia city, expect to do more of the work of building a social life yourselves, and expect to do more of that work in Spanish.

The murcianos themselves are genuinely warm people with a reputation for directness and generosity. The city has a festive culture that goes well beyond the famous Semana Santa processions — there are local festivals throughout the year, and the sense that public space is genuinely shared and enjoyed is palpable in the evenings when the Glorieta fills and the bar terraces come alive. Integrating into this takes language and patience, but it is not unwelcoming. It is simply authentic.

Getting to Murcia

Murcia-Corvera International Airport (airport code RMU) opened in 2019 to replace the old San Javier airport that had served the region for decades. It handles direct flights from multiple UK airports — Ryanair operates routes from London Stansted, Manchester, and other regional airports, with flight times of approximately 2.5 to 3 hours. The network continues to expand as the airport establishes itself, and low-cost connections to other European cities are increasingly available.

For those travelling within Spain, Murcia is connected to Madrid by high-speed AVE rail service in approximately 3.5 hours. The journey passes through attractive Castilian and Manchegan landscape and is considerably more comfortable than the equivalent road journey. Valencia is approximately 1.5 hours away by road, and Alicante is about 1 hour — both useful for the wider range of flights and services available at those larger airports.

Within the region, a car is a practical necessity for most expats. Public transport within Murcia city is functional, but connecting the city to the coastal areas and smaller towns requires your own transport. The roads are generally good, traffic outside the city centre manageable, and driving in Murcia is considerably less stressful than in larger Spanish cities.

Is Murcia Right for You?

Murcia will suit you well if you are looking for real Spain at genuinely affordable prices, if you are willing to invest in learning Spanish, and if you can accept — or even welcome — the fact that you will not be surrounded by other expats from day one. It is a region for people who want to build a life, not just to transplant a life. The city rewards engagement. The coast rewards patience and the willingness to look past the obvious resort towns to smaller, quieter places with more genuine character.

Murcia is not the right choice if your primary need is an established English-speaking social scene and expat community services that work out of the box. It is not Malaga. It is not Alicante. Those cities are excellent and have excellent reasons to be popular — and if that level of established infrastructure matters to you, you should go to one of them rather than expect Murcia to be something it is not. Equally, if the prospect of several months per year at 40°C genuinely concerns you, Murcia city in particular is a harder sell than the similarly-priced Alicante or Valencia to the north.

But for those who can work within its realities, Murcia offers something that is increasingly hard to find in Spain: low cost, high quality of life, genuine culture, excellent food, and the sense that you have discovered something before the rest of the world catches on. That sense may not last. The region's affordability and the quality of the Costa Cálida are not secrets that keep themselves. The time to take Murcia seriously is now.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Murcia a good place to live as an expat?
Yes, genuinely, if what you want is real value and authentic Spanish life rather than established expat infrastructure. Murcia city suits those who want to integrate into a Spanish city rather than a transplanted British community. La Manga and the Costa Cálida suit those who want beach proximity with English-speaking neighbours nearby. Murcia is not the right choice if you need a large English-speaking social scene from day one, or if you cannot handle extreme summer heat.
How much does it cost to live in Murcia, Spain?
Murcia is one of Spain's most affordable regions. A single person can live comfortably in Murcia city on approximately €1,200–€1,600 per month, covering rent, food, utilities, private health insurance, and leisure. The Costa Cálida costs are slightly higher at around €1,400–€1,800 per month for a comparable lifestyle, depending on whether you are renting year-round or in a seasonal coastal property.
Is Murcia city or the Costa Cálida better for expats?
It depends entirely on what you are looking for. Murcia city gives you urban amenities, a beautiful historic centre, cheaper rent, and authentic Spanish city life. The Costa Cálida, particularly La Manga del Mar Menor, offers beach proximity, a calmer pace, and an established English-speaking community. Many expats end up combining both — a base in the city and weekends on the coast. The two are around 45 minutes apart by car.
Do I need to speak Spanish to live in Murcia?
Yes, more so than in many major coastal tourist areas. In Murcia city and most inland areas, English is not widely spoken. You can manage without Spanish, but daily life will be considerably easier and richer with even basic conversational Spanish. On the Costa Cálida, particularly La Manga del Mar Menor, English is widely spoken due to the established British community. For visa applications, empadronamiento, and dealings with local authorities anywhere in Murcia, Spanish is essentially required.
What is the summer weather like in Murcia?
Hot — genuinely hot. Murcia has one of Europe's most intense summer climates. July and August regularly see temperatures above 40°C, sometimes reaching 43–44°C. Mornings and evenings are more bearable, and Spaniards adapt their rhythms accordingly — late lunches, long afternoons indoors, evening activity after 7pm. The coast is cooled somewhat by sea breeze. Winter is mild, typically 10–16°C, and spring and autumn are genuinely beautiful. Summer heat is a real consideration and not something to minimise in your planning.
How do I get to Murcia from the UK?
Murcia-Corvera Airport (RMU) has direct flights from multiple UK airports, including Ryanair routes from London Stansted, Manchester, and others. Flight time is approximately 2.5–3 hours. By high-speed AVE train, Murcia is approximately 3.5 hours from Madrid. The airport opened in 2019 and replaced the old San Javier airport, with connections improving as the route network develops.

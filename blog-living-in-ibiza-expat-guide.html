Living in Ibiza as an Expat: Beyond the Party, Into the Life

Come to Ibiza in February and you'll find something the July crowds never see: a small island going about its own life. The clubs are closed. The beach bars are shuttered. The bays are dramatically quiet. What you find instead is a community — artists, wellness practitioners, remote workers, retirees, long-term families — living a Mediterranean island existence that has very little to do with the thing that put Ibiza on the map.

Ibiza is one of Europe's most misunderstood places to live. Ask someone who has never been and they'll describe a neon-lit carnival of excess — an island that exists purely for a certain kind of summer hedonism. Ask someone who has lived there year-round for five years and they will likely describe the most beautiful, particular, and occasionally maddening place they have ever called home.

The truth is that Ibiza contains multitudes. It is the party island — that reputation is real and earned. But it is also an island with a 2,000-year-old Phoenician capital (a UNESCO World Heritage Site), a thriving year-round community of international creative people, one of the most physically beautiful environments in the Mediterranean, and a social ecosystem that is unlike anything you will find on the Spanish mainland. Understanding both sides of Ibiza — and especially the gap between the two — is essential before you decide whether it is the right home for you.

Ibiza Beyond the Nightlife: The Real Year-Round Community

The year-round expat population in Ibiza is genuinely diverse. You will find British families who moved here for the schools and the sea; German artists who discovered the north of the island in the 1970s and never left; Dutch wellness practitioners running yoga retreats; French chefs who built restaurants; tech workers who chose the island as their remote base; and long-established retirees who want nothing to do with the clubs and everything to do with the hiking, the sailing, and the extraordinary light.

What binds this community is not a shared lifestyle so much as a shared appreciation for what the island itself offers: an extraordinary natural environment (pine forests, turquoise coves, red rock cliffs), a genuinely international social scene, a pace of life that is slower and more deliberate than any major European city, and a sense of being somewhere genuinely special. People who live in Ibiza year-round are rarely indifferent to it. They have made a conscious choice, and they know why.

What draws people here is rarely the clubs. It is almost always the island itself.

The Four Ibizas: Which Community Is Yours?

Ibiza Town (Eivissa)

The capital is the most complete year-round city on the island. The old town — the Dalt Vila, a walled citadel perched on a hill above the harbour — is a UNESCO World Heritage Site of genuine architectural beauty: narrow medieval lanes, Renaissance ramparts, sweeping views over the bay. Below it, the port area and the modern town have developed into a genuinely lively urban environment with excellent restaurants, a vibrant arts scene, regular markets, and enough of the infrastructure of normal urban life to make year-round living practical.

Ibiza Town is where most working expats and digital nomads settle — it has the best internet infrastructure, the most services, and the most to do in winter. It is also where you will find the best restaurants (year-round, not just seasonal), the most active nightlife outside summer months, and the greatest concentration of international residents. If you want to feel the island's pulse in every season, Ibiza Town is the right base.

San Antonio (Sant Antoni)

San Antonio has long been Ibiza's most maligned town — associated with cheap package tourism, British lads' holidays, and the kind of nightlife that makes the more refined quarters of Ibiza Town wince. That reputation, while historically earned, is becoming outdated. The town has invested in itself over the past decade: the sunset strip has moved upmarket, the food scene has improved, and the residential areas surrounding the centre offer genuine affordability by Ibiza standards.

For expats, San Antonio suits those who want easy access to the west coast beaches (some of Ibiza's finest sunset spots are nearby — Café del Mar's location is not a coincidence) and who prioritise value over cachet. The town centre can still be exhaustingly loud in summer, but the surrounding villas and quieter residential streets are perfectly liveable. Rents here are typically the most affordable on the island.

Santa Eulalia (Santa Eulàlia del Riu)

The quietest and most family-friendly of Ibiza's main towns, Santa Eulalia sits on the east coast and has the largest established British expat community on the island. It has a proper town feel — a marina, a pleasant seafront promenade, good schools, a weekly market, and year-round restaurants and services that remain open even in the quietest winter months. The pace here is noticeably different from Ibiza Town: more settled, more residential, more self-contained.

Santa Eulalia is where Ibiza's long-term retiree community is most concentrated, and where families with children tend to gravitate. The schools are good, the beaches are calm and safe, and the town has enough services to make daily life comfortable without requiring constant trips to Ibiza Town. If you are coming to Ibiza for a quiet, beautiful life rather than cultural intensity, Santa Eulalia is almost certainly the right answer.

The North: Las Dalias, San Juan, Sant Llorenç

The north of Ibiza is a different island. Here, the landscape becomes more rugged — terracotta hills, dry stone walls, ancient fincas half-hidden among carob trees. The hippie market at Las Dalias has been running since 1985 and remains a genuine community institution. The villages of San Juan (Sant Joan de Labritja) and the surrounding countryside attract Ibiza's most bohemian residents: artists, musicians, yoga teachers, organic farmers, and people who came for a season in 1994 and stayed.

Internet connectivity in rural northern Ibiza can be patchy — fibre has improved significantly but does not reach everywhere. Life here demands a car, patience with the limitations of island supply chains, and an affection for rural solitude that not everyone has in them. For those who do, the north of Ibiza offers something genuinely rare in Europe: a beautiful, unspoilt, strongly communal rural environment within 40 minutes of an international airport.

Cost of Living in Ibiza: An Honest Picture

Ibiza is expensive. It is more expensive than most of mainland Spain, and meaningfully more expensive than comparably sized Spanish cities like Alicante or Murcia. Here is why, and what it costs:

The island's supply chain adds cost to almost everything — groceries, building materials, services. Rents are high, pushed upward by the enormous summer holiday lettings market. Many landlords prefer lucrative short-term holiday lets over long-term residential tenancies, which shrinks the available stock for year-round residents and keeps prices elevated.

Realistic monthly costs for 2026

  • Rent, one-bedroom apartment in Ibiza Town: €900–€1,400
  • Rent, one-bedroom apartment in Santa Eulalia: €850–€1,250
  • Rent, rural finca or cottage (north): €800–€1,400 (highly variable by size and condition)
  • Groceries: €300–€400 per person (noticeably higher than mainland)
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet): €130–€200
  • Private health insurance: €100–€280 depending on age
  • Car (essential): €200–€350 per month including insurance and fuel
  • Dining out and leisure: €300–€600 (varies enormously by lifestyle)
  • Comfortable single-person total: approximately €2,000–€2,900 per month

In summer, prices spike for many services and any leisure activity. If your lifestyle includes beach clubs, boat trips, or the island's famous restaurant scene in high season, add significantly to those figures. Ibiza rewards those who adapt their habits to the rhythms of island life — cooking at home from the local markets, saving the beach clubs for occasions rather than routines.

The Seasonal Reality: Two Islands in One

This is the most important thing to understand about Ibiza, and the thing most people do not adequately anticipate before moving: the island is two completely different places depending on the season.

June through September transforms Ibiza into one of Europe's most frenetic tourist destinations. The island's population of around 150,000 swells massively with visitors. Traffic on the single main road around the island can become genuinely gridlocked. Beaches fill completely by mid-morning. Prices spike across restaurants, services, and supermarkets. The noise — particularly around San Antonio and certain coastal areas — can make sleeping with windows open impossible. Even those parts of the island that remain relatively insulated (the north, quiet residential neighbourhoods) feel the pressure.

Year-round residents navigate this differently. Some genuinely love the energy — the summer explosion of life is one of the things that drew them here, and they embrace it. Others retreat: they shop early, avoid certain roads, take their holidays off-island in July and August, and wait for the blessed relief of October, when the island exhales.

November through March is almost the opposite. Around 40–50% of businesses reduce their hours significantly or close entirely for winter. The promenades that were crowded in August feel contemplative and almost private. The island is green (Ibiza's winters bring enough rain to keep the landscape vivid), mild (rarely below 12°C), and often extraordinarily beautiful. Long walks through pine forests to empty coves. Unhurried dinners at the few restaurants that stay open year-round. A genuine sense of the island belonging to those who chose to stay.

Many people who move to Ibiza fall in love with the winter. Some find it too quiet — the lack of services, the social contraction, and the physical isolation of island life without the summer stimulation feel oppressive. Knowing which kind of person you are before you move is genuinely important.

Healthcare in Ibiza

Healthcare is one area where Ibiza's island status creates real limitations. The primary public facility is Hospital Can Misses in Ibiza Town — a general hospital with emergency services and most standard specialties. For routine and moderate-complexity care, it functions well. For serious or complex specialist needs, transfer to mainland Spain (Valencia or Barcelona, both reachable within an hour by air or several hours by ferry) may be necessary.

Private healthcare is covered by Policlínica Nuestra Señora del Rosario, also in Ibiza Town, which provides good general and specialist care for routine needs. A number of English-speaking GPs and specialists operate privately on the island, particularly in the areas with large expat populations.

Non-EU residents on an NLV or DNV must hold private health insurance — this is both a visa requirement and a practical necessity. Island residents should ensure their policy includes provisions for medical evacuation or transport to mainland facilities in case of serious illness or injury requiring specialist intervention. Not all standard Spanish private health policies include this by default.

Getting Around Ibiza: The Car Question

A car is essential for living in Ibiza. The island has limited public transport — buses connect the main towns but routes are infrequent and do not serve rural areas at all. The physical layout of the island, with its dispersed communities and attractions spread across forested hillsides and coastal roads, means that daily life without a car quickly becomes very restricted.

In Ibiza Town itself, you can manage many daily tasks on foot or by bicycle. In Santa Eulalia, the town centre is walkable. But for reaching the beaches (particularly the more beautiful ones, which are not served by bus), shopping at large supermarkets, visiting the north, or accessing healthcare, a car moves from helpful to necessary.

Summer driving deserves specific mention: the main roads, particularly the PM-810 between Ibiza Town and San Antonio, can be severely congested in July and August. Plan ahead, travel early or late, and learn the secondary roads. Scooters and motorcycles are popular with many residents for navigating summer traffic.

Visas for Non-EU Expats in Ibiza

Ibiza is part of Spain, so the same national visa framework applies as on the mainland. Non-EU citizens planning to live here long-term need a Spanish residence visa applied for before arrival. The two main options for the Ibiza expat community are:

  • Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) — for retirees and those living on passive income (pensions, investments, rental income). Requires demonstrating income of approximately €28,800 per year for a single applicant, plus private health insurance and other documentation.
  • Digital Nomad Visa (DNV) — for those working remotely for companies or clients outside Spain. Requires demonstrating income of approximately €2,300+ per month from remote work, plus private health insurance.

Both visas are applied for at your Spanish consulate in your home country before you move. The residency administration for Ibiza is handled through the Balearic Islands regional offices — your NIE application and TIE card registration will be processed on-island at the Comisaría de Policía in Ibiza Town.

Community and Social Life

One of Ibiza's genuine surprises for new arrivals is how welcoming and interconnected the year-round community is. The island is small enough that people from very different backgrounds and lifestyles inevitably meet. The expat community here is unusually integrated compared to, say, the Costa del Sol — largely because the island's scale forces a kind of proximity that a large coastal city does not.

Social life on Ibiza tends to happen around food markets (the Mercado de las Dalias, the Mercado de Santa Gertrudis, the Santa Eulalia market), community events, beach gatherings in spring and autumn, and the island's genuinely excellent restaurant scene. There are expat groups, language exchanges, yoga communities, music circles, and neighbourhood associations covering practical matters. Making friends on Ibiza tends to happen faster than in larger, more anonymous cities.

The island's creative community is a particular draw. Artists, musicians, writers, and photographers have been drawn to Ibiza's light and landscape for decades. If that is your world, you will find your people here relatively quickly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Ibiza a good place to live as an expat?
Yes — for the right kind of person. Ibiza has a well-established, diverse year-round expat community of artists, creatives, wellness practitioners, remote workers, retirees, and families. The island offers a genuine Mediterranean lifestyle: beautiful natural environment, great food, a warm and interesting social scene, and access to some of Europe's most beautiful beaches. It is expensive by Spanish standards, winter brings a noticeable quiet, and healthcare options are more limited than on the mainland. People who thrive here typically accept these trade-offs in exchange for the unique quality of island life.
How expensive is it to live in Ibiza?
Ibiza is one of the more expensive places to live in Spain. A one-bedroom apartment in Ibiza Town typically costs €900–€1,400 per month; rural properties in the north run €800–€1,200. Groceries are more expensive than the Spanish mainland due to island supply chains. A comfortable single-person lifestyle costs €2,000–€2,800 per month, excluding significant travel.
What is Ibiza like in winter?
Very different from summer. From November through March, around half of businesses reduce hours or close entirely, and the tourist crowds are completely absent. The weather is mild (12–18°C), the pace slows dramatically, and the island takes on a more genuinely local character. Many year-round residents love winter in Ibiza; others find it too quiet. This is the single most important thing to understand before committing to living there.
What are the best areas in Ibiza for expats?
Ibiza Town offers the most services and the best year-round social scene — the best choice for working expats. Santa Eulalia is most popular for families and retirees — quieter, family-friendly, with a large British expat community. The north attracts bohemian, wellness-oriented residents seeking rural peace. San Antonio suits those wanting affordability and west coast access.
What visa do I need to live in Ibiza as a non-EU citizen?
Ibiza is part of Spain (the Balearic Islands), so the same national visa system applies. Retirees living on passive income typically apply for the Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV). Remote workers employed by foreign companies apply for the Digital Nomad Visa (DNV). Both are applied for at your Spanish consulate before you move.
Is healthcare adequate in Ibiza for year-round residents?
For routine healthcare, yes. Hospital Can Misses in Ibiza Town provides public emergency and general healthcare, and Policlínica Nuestra Señora del Rosario covers private needs. For complex specialist care, mainland Spain may be necessary. Non-EU residents on NLV or DNV must hold private health insurance, ideally including medical evacuation provisions.

DIGITAL NOMAD GUIDE

Digital Nomad Visa San Sebastián: Small City, Big Life, Serious Food

San Sebastián is not the biggest nomad hub in Spain. It is something more useful: one of the best places in Europe to actually live while working remotely. This is the guide for people who care more about quality of life than the size of the coworking scene.

Home / Blog / Digital Nomad Visa San Sebastián: Small City, Big Life, Serious Food

Most digital nomad guides organise Spain by scale. Barcelona at the top, then Valencia, then Las Palmas, then a cluster of mid-sized cities. San Sebastián — Donostia in Basque, the capital of Gipuzkoa province in the Basque Country — rarely appears high on those lists, and that is a genuine oversight. At roughly 187,000 people, it is a small city by Spanish standards. But small is not the same as limited. San Sebastián has more Michelin-starred restaurants per capita than almost anywhere on Earth, a beach you can walk to from the old town in ten minutes, fast Atlantic weather that keeps summers bearable, a creative and industrial culture rooted in the Basque Country's distinctive character, and infrastructure that works. It is not for nomads who need a scene. It is for nomads who want to live well.

The practical question is whether the quality of life translates into a functional remote work environment. The answer is yes — with a clear understanding of the trade-offs. The coworking scene is modest. The international nomad community is smaller than Barcelona or Las Palmas. Rent is higher than the Canary Islands or Andalusia. But the internet is fast, the city is entirely walkable, the food culture is genuinely extraordinary rather than merely good, and the climate means you will not lose August to heat-induced inertia. What follows is the complete picture.

Why San Sebastián — Not Scale, But Quality

The case for San Sebastián is not built on nomad infrastructure. It is built on the quality of everyday life, which is a different and arguably more important consideration for someone planning to live somewhere for a year or more.

Start with the physical environment. La Concha — the crescent bay at the heart of the city — is one of the most beautiful urban beaches in Europe, and it is a genuine ten-minute walk from the Parte Vieja (old town). This is not a weekend excursion. It is a lunchtime option. The city is compact enough — the Parte Vieja to the Gros neighbourhood on foot takes fifteen minutes — that owning a car is unnecessary and getting anywhere by walking is realistic. This removes a category of friction that larger cities impose by default.

The climate is Atlantic rather than Mediterranean. Summers in San Sebastián run 18–22°C in July and August. Mild, occasionally cloudy, sometimes rainy — but nothing like the 38°C heat that makes working in Seville or Madrid in August an air-conditioning-dependent activity. For remote workers, the practical implication is that your apartment does not need to be a fortress against the heat and your outdoor hours are not compressed into early morning and late evening.

The Basque Country has a distinct cultural and economic identity within Spain. The Mondragon cooperative corporation — one of the world's largest worker-owned industrial enterprises — is based in Gipuzkoa. Aerospace engineering firm SENER, defence and energy companies, and a growing technology and startup ecosystem all operate in the region. Tabakalera, the former tobacco factory in the centre of San Sebastián converted into a major creative and cultural hub, is evidence of what that industrial confidence looks like when it turns toward the creative economy. This is not a city that survives on tourism. It has its own substance.

The food requires its own paragraph because it is not peripheral to the quality of life argument — it is central to it. Pintxos (the Basque version of tapas, typically served on bread and priced individually at the bar) are the functional currency of evening socialising in San Sebastián. Eating in the bars of the Parte Vieja or Gros neighbourhood is genuinely affordable despite the city's culinary reputation, because pintxos culture is democratic: you eat standing at the bar, you move between bars, and the total bill for a remarkable evening of food and wine is far less than a comparable meal at a sit-down restaurant. The Michelin-starred restaurants exist. So do the bars where a glass of txakoli and three pintxos costs €6. Both are part of the same food culture.

Coworking and Working Spaces

San Sebastián's coworking scene is functional rather than extensive. There are no dozens of competing spaces. What exists is good.

Tabakalera

The most distinctive working environment in the city. Tabakalera is a vast former tobacco factory on the edge of the Parte Vieja that has been converted into a multi-disciplinary cultural centre housing cinema, exhibition space, creative studios, event spaces, and coworking areas. The architecture is industrial and considered — high ceilings, natural light, a building that gives the sense of being inside something that matters. The people working there tend to be from the Basque creative and technology industries: filmmakers, designers, engineers, researchers. Day passes and monthly memberships are available. If you want a coworking environment that feels like it has a reason to exist beyond square metres and a coffee machine, Tabakalera is it.

BIC Gipuzkoa

The Basque Innovation Centre — a professional coworking and business incubation space oriented toward technology and innovation businesses. The atmosphere is more corporate than Tabakalera, the membership more likely to include people building companies than freelancers. Fast, reliable internet, meeting room access, and a serious working environment. The right choice if your work involves regular client calls and you need guaranteed connectivity and quiet.

HubEkin and Others

HubEkin operates as a collaborative working space with a community focus. Several other smaller options exist in the city. The total number of dedicated coworking spaces is modest — San Sebastián is not Valencia or Madrid in this regard — but for a city of 187,000 people with a real technology and creative sector, the provision is adequate.

Working from Cafés

The café culture in Gros — the neighbourhood east of the Urumea river, slightly younger and more local in character than the Parte Vieja — and in the Parte Vieja itself is genuinely conducive to working. Many of the bars and cafés have Wi-Fi, seating that accommodates laptops, and a culture of extended occupation. Morning coffee and a croissant at a bar in Gros while taking notes or finishing a document before your first call is not a theoretical possibility — it is a common reality for people living in the city. This is not the case everywhere in Spain, where café culture often moves at a pace that discourages lingering work sessions.

Internet and Connectivity

Spain has among the best fibre broadband infrastructure in Europe, and San Sebastián is fully served. Most apartments in the city can access 300–600 Mbps symmetrical fibre from providers including Movistar, Vodafone, Orange, and MásMóvil. The speeds are real — not headline figures that collapse under load — and installation is typically straightforward for residents with a rental contract.

5G mobile coverage is reliable throughout the city. Movistar and Orange have strong 5G presence in San Sebastián; 4G+ is universal. Spanish SIM cards are easy to obtain and pay-monthly data plans are competitively priced. For anyone who needs mobile connectivity as a backup to their fixed broadband — or for working from locations without Wi-Fi — the mobile network is robust.

Connectivity is not a concern for remote workers choosing San Sebastián. It is not a city where you will be managing workarounds for slow infrastructure. The Basque Country has an industrially serious approach to infrastructure, and the internet reflects that.

The Timezone Advantage

San Sebastián operates on CET — UTC+1 in winter, UTC+2 in summer. This is one of the most practically useful timezones for remote workers with European or US East Coast clients.

  • European clients (Germany, Netherlands, France, Scandinavia): same timezone. No friction, no adjustment.
  • UK clients: one hour ahead. Morning meetings at 9am San Sebastián time begin at 8am London time — workable and never awkward.
  • US East Coast clients: 6 hours ahead in summer, 5 hours in winter. Starting your working day at 7am gives you solid coverage of US East Coast business hours through the morning. A common pattern: deep work from 7–11am, US calls from 11am–2pm, afternoon free.
  • US West Coast clients: 8–9 hours ahead. Requires early morning availability or an async-first working relationship. Manageable with planning, but requires deliberate scheduling.

For most European and US East Coast remote workers, CET is a genuine asset. The morning hours in San Sebastián — before European colleagues have hit their stride and while US East Coast colleagues are finishing their previous day — are productive hours without external pull on your attention.

What Does It Cost to Live as a Nomad in San Sebastián?

San Sebastián is the most expensive city in this guide relative to the rest of Spain. It is worth being precise about what that means in practice.

Typical Monthly Budget

  • Rent, one-bedroom apartment (Gros, Amara, or Antiguo): €1,000–€1,500/month
  • Utilities including fast fibre internet: €100–€150/month
  • Coworking membership or regular café working: €100–€200/month
  • Food — groceries plus regular pintxos evenings: €350–€550/month
  • Transport (walking city — minimal): €30–€60/month
  • Private health insurance (DNV requirement): €80–€150/month
  • Total: approximately €1,660–€2,610/month, with most nomads settling at €2,000–€2,800

For context: a comparable nomad budget in London is £3,500–£5,000 per month. In Amsterdam, €3,000–€4,200. In Zurich, the figure is considerably higher. San Sebastián at €2,000–€2,800 is mid-market by western European standards, not expensive. You pay more than you would in Murcia or Alicante, and you receive something qualitatively different in return.

The food budget deserves a note. Pintxos culture offsets the cost of eating in a city with a significant fine-dining reputation. Eating well in San Sebastián — properly, enjoyably well — does not require going to restaurants with tasting menus. It requires going to bars in the Parte Vieja or Gros with a glass of txakoli and eating pintxos at the counter. This is the way the city eats, and it costs €15–€30 for a genuinely excellent evening. Factor that into the food column and the overall budget becomes more comfortable than the headline rent figure suggests.

Spain's Digital Nomad Visa — The Requirements

Spain's Digital Nomad Visa (DNV), introduced under the Startups Law, is open to non-EU nationals working remotely for employers or clients based outside Spain. The core requirements for 2026:

  • Income threshold: at least 200% of Spain's IPREM — approximately €2,800 per month in 2026. Self-employed applicants must demonstrate 3x IPREM. Income must be evidenced through employment contracts, invoices, bank statements, or a combination.
  • Foreign income source: at least 80% of income must derive from clients or employers based outside Spain. A small proportion of Spanish-sourced income (up to 20%) is permitted.
  • Employment or freelance proof: employees need a contract confirming the remote nature of the role; freelancers need to demonstrate an active client base, typically through contracts or recent invoices.
  • Private health insurance: comprehensive cover valid in Spain, with no co-payments and no coverage gaps. Must be with an insurer authorised to operate in Spain.
  • Criminal record certificate: apostilled, issued within the past three to six months, from all countries of residence over the past five years.
  • Proof of accommodation in Spain: a rental contract or property deed in your name.

The DNV is applied for at a Spanish consulate in your home country before you move. Processing times vary by consulate — typically 20–45 working days for a complete application. The visa is initially valid for one year, renewable for two-year periods. After five continuous years of legal residence, long-term EU residency becomes available.

Beckham Law (Ley Beckham): qualifying DNV holders can elect a flat 24% tax rate on Spanish-sourced income for up to five years, rather than the standard progressive income tax bands. Whether this is advantageous depends on your total income and structure. Take professional tax advice before applying — the rules around what constitutes Spanish-sourced income matter significantly here.

Day-to-Day Nomad Life in San Sebastián

The rhythm of working life in San Sebastián is genuinely different from a larger city, and the difference is mostly in your favour. The city is small enough that you know your neighbourhood within a week. The bar where you take your morning coffee, the route to the coworking space, the particular pintxos bar in the Parte Vieja where the bacalao is reliably excellent — these become fixed points quickly, and fixed points reduce the cognitive load of daily life in a way that matters when you are also trying to do productive work.

A common pattern: start at a Gros café by 8am, coffee and a light breakfast, first two to three hours of focused work before European colleagues reach peak activity. Walk to Tabakalera or BIC Gipuzkoa for calls and collaborative work through mid-morning. La Concha at lunchtime on a clear day — fifteen minutes on foot from most central locations, a genuine lunch break rather than a desk-eating exercise. Afternoon calls and deep work, finishing by 5–6pm. Evening: Parte Vieja for pintxos, a glass of txakoli, the particular social ritual of moving between bars that characterises Basque evening culture.

On free afternoons, Zurriola beach — on the Gros side of the river, more exposed and popular with surfers — runs surf lessons throughout the season. The surrounding countryside offers hiking in the hills above the city within thirty minutes on foot. The French border is an hour by car or train; San Sebastián to Biarritz is a practical weekend. The geography gives the city a sense of possibility without requiring it to be anything other than itself.

The compactness is worth emphasising separately. Walking to everything — from your apartment to the coworking space, from the coworking space to the beach, from the beach to the pintxos bars — removes the transit cost that larger cities impose. In Barcelona you take the metro. In Madrid you take the metro. In San Sebastián you walk, and what you see on the walk is worth seeing.

Finding Your Community

The nomad community in San Sebastián is smaller than Barcelona's or Las Palmas's. That is accurate and worth knowing upfront. It is not, however, thin. The people working at Tabakalera form a genuine creative community — filmmakers, designers, technologists, researchers connected to the Basque cultural and industrial ecosystem. International students and faculty at the University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU) campus in San Sebastián bring an international population with an academic and research orientation. The growing Basque startup scene, centred on the Basque Country's industrial base but increasingly extending into technology, provides a professional network that extends beyond the nomad category.

Connection through food culture is not incidental in San Sebastián — it is the primary social mechanism. The pintxos bar is the Basque equivalent of the pub or the café, and its social function is real. Showing up regularly at the same bars in Gros or the Parte Vieja means becoming a recognisable face, which in a city of 187,000 people happens faster than it does in a city of two million. This is not a replacement for deliberate community-building, but it is a genuine foundation for it.

For nomads who want the support of an organised international community from day one, Barcelona or Las Palmas will serve them better. For nomads who are comfortable finding their footing through professional spaces, neighbourhood rhythms, and the particular social architecture of Basque food culture — San Sebastián rewards patience with something more durable than a meetup schedule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is San Sebastián a good city for digital nomads?
Yes, for the right kind of nomad. If you want a large nomad meetup scene, dozens of coworking options, and constant social events with remote workers, look at Las Palmas, Barcelona, or Valencia. If you want to live exceptionally well in a small, sophisticated city while working productively — excellent food, fast internet, a beautiful beach, no brutal summers, walkable everywhere — San Sebastián is outstanding.
What coworking spaces are in San Sebastián?
The main options are Tabakalera (the city's creative and cultural hub, with coworking space in the former tobacco factory — a remarkable environment), BIC Gipuzkoa (professional innovation hub), and HubEkin. Several cafés in Gros and the Parte Vieja function well as informal working spaces. The coworking scene is modest in size but genuine in quality.
How fast is internet in San Sebastián?
Very fast. Spain has among the best fibre broadband infrastructure in Europe, and San Sebastián is fully covered. Most apartments can access 300–600 Mbps fibre, and 5G mobile coverage is reliable throughout the city. Connectivity is not a concern for remote workers.
What are the Digital Nomad Visa income requirements for 2026?
You must demonstrate income of at least 200% of Spain's IPREM — approximately €2,800 per month in 2026. Self-employed workers need to show 3x IPREM. At least 80% of your income must come from clients or an employer outside Spain. You also need private health insurance and a clean criminal record.
Is San Sebastián expensive for digital nomads?
By Spanish standards, yes. Expect to spend €2,000–€2,800 per month including rent (€1,000–€1,500 for a one-bedroom), food, internet, and health insurance. By northern European or North American standards, this is moderate. The food quality you get for this budget — including eating in Michelin-starred-region pintxos bars regularly — is exceptional. It is a trade: you pay more than Alicante, but you live better than almost anywhere.
What is the Beckham Law and does it apply to DNV holders?
The Beckham Law (officially the Ley de Startups provision for digital nomads) allows qualifying DNV holders to opt into a flat 24% income tax rate on Spanish-sourced income for up to five years, rather than the standard progressive Spanish income tax bands. Whether this is advantageous depends on your income level and circumstances. Take professional tax advice before applying.

Spanish Mortgages for Non-Residents: Rates, LTV Limits and How to Apply

Getting a mortgage in Spain as a non-resident is absolutely possible — but the terms differ from what Spanish residents receive. Here is exactly what to expect, which banks to approach, and how to put together a strong application.

Buying property in Spain as a foreigner is a popular goal for retirees, second-home buyers and investors from the UK, US, Canada, Australia and South Africa. The good news is that Spain imposes no restrictions on property ownership by non-nationals, and most major Spanish banks actively court international buyers. The less obvious news is that non-resident mortgage terms are meaningfully different from those available to Spanish residents — and understanding those differences before you start your property search will save you both time and money.

One important point before we go further: purchasing a property in Spain does not grant you the right to live there. If you are a non-EU citizen (including UK nationals since Brexit), you are still subject to the 90-day Schengen limit unless you hold a valid Spanish residency visa. We cover the visa options — including the Non-Lucrative Visa and the Digital Nomad Visa — separately, and we can handle both the property purchase and the visa application simultaneously.

What Makes a Non-Resident Mortgage Different?

Spanish banks classify you as a non-resident borrower if you spend fewer than 183 days per year in Spain or if your primary income and economic interests are based outside the country. That classification triggers a different underwriting framework in three key areas:

Loan-to-Value (LTV) Limits

This is the most significant practical difference. Spanish residents can borrow up to 80% — and occasionally 90% — of a property's appraised value. Non-residents are typically capped at 60% to 70% LTV. In practice this means you need a deposit of 30% to 40%, plus an additional 10% to 13% to cover purchase taxes and legal fees. For a €300,000 property, plan to have at least €120,000 to €150,000 in liquid savings before you begin.

Interest Rates and Spreads

Non-resident borrowers generally pay a slightly higher spread over Euribor than residents do, reflecting the additional compliance costs lenders incur. On a variable-rate deal, expect spreads of around Euribor plus 1.5% to 2.0% rather than the Euribor plus 1.0% that a well-qualified resident might achieve. Fixed-rate offers for non-residents typically sit between 3.2% and 3.5% for a 20-year term. That said, rates move with the ECB cycle and with individual bank competition, so it always pays to compare multiple offers.

Maximum Term and Age Restrictions

Most Spanish lenders cap non-resident mortgages at 20 to 25 years, and they require the loan to be fully repaid by the time the borrower reaches age 65 or 75 depending on the bank. A 58-year-old applicant will therefore only be offered a 7 to 17-year term at most lenders, which pushes monthly repayments higher.

Which Banks Lend to Non-Residents?

All of Spain's major retail banks offer non-resident mortgage products, though the depth of their English-language support and the speed of their international underwriting varies. The five names foreign buyers encounter most frequently are:

  • Banco Santander — operates dedicated international mortgage services with English-language support and an online pre-qualification tool.
  • BBVA — offers a fully digital application process and publishes its non-resident mortgage conditions in English on its website.
  • CaixaBank — has a dedicated HolaBank service for international clients, accepts initial documentation in your home language and provides a viability assessment within 72 hours.
  • Bankinter — popular with UK and US buyers; offers both fixed and mixed-rate structures explicitly marketed to foreign non-residents.
  • Banco Sabadell — publishes a step-by-step guide to its non-resident mortgage process in English, French and German and has handled a large volume of international transactions.

Regional savings banks (cajas) occasionally offer competitive rates, and specialist mortgage brokers operating in the Spanish market can sometimes negotiate terms that beat the headline rates from the big five. An independent broker is particularly useful if your income is self-employed or comes from multiple sources.

What Documents Will the Bank Ask For?

Non-resident mortgage applications require more documentation than a typical domestic loan, because the bank must verify your income and creditworthiness through foreign records rather than Spanish tax filings. You will typically need to provide:

  • Your NIE (Número de Identificación de Extranjero) — this foreign tax identification number is mandatory before you can open a Spanish bank account, pay property taxes or sign any property deed. You can apply at a Spanish consulate in your home country.
  • Passport and proof of home address (a recent utility bill or bank statement).
  • The last three to six months of payslips if you are employed, or the last two years of certified tax returns and audited accounts if you are self-employed.
  • Bank statements for the past three to six months, demonstrating savings, regular income deposits and the source of your deposit funds.
  • A credit report from your home country's main credit bureau (such as Experian or Equifax in the UK, or a credit transcript for US applicants).
  • US citizens must also provide Form W-9 and IRS tax transcripts to comply with FATCA regulations.
  • The preliminary purchase agreement or reservation contract for the property you intend to buy.

All foreign documents must be translated by an official sworn translator (traductor jurado). Documents from outside the EU will also need an apostille certification to be accepted by Spanish institutions.

The Costs You Need to Budget For

Spain's 2019 Mortgage Credit Law shifted most of the closing costs onto the lender rather than the buyer, which reduced upfront expenses significantly. As a non-resident buyer the costs you are responsible for include:

  • Property valuation fee (tasación): typically €300 to €500.
  • Property registry report (nota simple): €8 to €20.
  • Mortgage arrangement fee: some lenders charge 0.5% to 1.5% of the loan amount; others waive this entirely.
  • Home insurance (required by the lender): typically €200 to €400 for the first year.

The bank pays the notary, land registry and stamp duty (AJD) costs that were previously borne by buyers. On a €200,000 mortgage this can represent a saving of €2,000 to €3,500 compared with the pre-2019 regime.

Currency Risk: A Point UK, US and Australian Buyers Often Overlook

If your income is in pounds sterling, US dollars or Australian dollars, your monthly mortgage payment in euros will fluctuate in real terms every time exchange rates move. A strengthening euro against your home currency increases your effective repayment cost without any change to the nominal euro amount. Before committing to a variable-rate euro mortgage, it is worth considering whether you can lock in a forward exchange rate for your deposit transfer and whether your financial planning accounts for a 10% to 15% adverse currency move over the life of the loan.

How to Improve Your Application

The single biggest lever is your loan-to-value ratio. Increasing your deposit from 30% to 35% or 40% can reduce your interest rate by 10 to 20 basis points and make the difference between approval and rejection at more conservative lenders. Paying down existing debts before applying will also improve your debt-to-income ratio — Spanish banks typically want your total monthly debt obligations (existing loans plus the new mortgage) to stay below 35% of your net monthly income. Opening a Spanish current account and routing your rental or pension income through it several months before you apply demonstrates financial stability and sometimes earns a small rate discount.

Property Ownership and Residency: The Key Distinction

We want to be direct about this because it catches many buyers off guard. Buying a property in Spain — regardless of its price — does not automatically give you the right to live there for more than 90 days in any 180-day period. The Schengen rules apply to non-EU nationals whether they own a property or not. If you want to spend extended time in Spain, or retire there permanently, you will need a Spanish visa in addition to your property purchase. The Non-Lucrative Visa is the most popular route for retirees and passive income earners; the Digital Nomad Visa suits remote workers and freelancers. Frequently Asked Questions

Can a non-resident get a mortgage in Spain?
Yes. Non-residents can obtain Spanish mortgages from most major banks. The key differences are a lower loan-to-value cap (typically 60–70%) and more extensive documentation requirements. You will need an NIE before you can complete any property transaction.
How much deposit do I need for a Spanish mortgage as a non-resident?
Budget for a deposit of 30–40% of the purchase price. On top of that, allow 10–13% for purchase taxes, notary fees and legal costs. For a €300,000 property, you should have at least €120,000–€150,000 in liquid funds available.
Which Spanish banks offer mortgages to foreigners?
All major Spanish banks lend to non-residents: Santander, BBVA, CaixaBank, Bankinter and Banco Sabadell all have dedicated international mortgage products and English-language support teams.
Does buying property in Spain give me residency?
No. Property ownership does not grant residency rights. Non-EU nationals (including UK citizens post-Brexit) are still subject to the 90-day Schengen limit. You need a separate Spanish visa — such as the Non-Lucrative Visa or Digital Nomad Visa — to live in Spain long-term.
Do I need an NIE to get a Spanish mortgage?
Yes. An NIE is required before you can open a Spanish bank account, pay property taxes or sign a mortgage deed. You can apply at a Spanish consulate in your home country or at a police station in Spain. We can manage this process for you as part of a combined property and visa service.
What is the maximum mortgage term for non-residents in Spain?
Most lenders cap non-resident mortgage terms at 20–25 years and require the loan to be fully repaid by the time the borrower reaches age 65–75. Older buyers will therefore face shorter maximum terms and higher monthly payments.

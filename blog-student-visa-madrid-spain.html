Student Visa Madrid: Universities, Costs, and Life as an International Student

Madrid is home to some of Europe's most respected universities and has one of Spain's largest international student populations. Here's the practical information you need to make the right choice — and get the visa sorted.

Home / Blog / Student Visa Madrid: Universities, Costs, and Life as an International Student

Madrid is not the obvious first choice for international students who think of Spain — many picture Barcelona or the beach cities of the south. But Madrid's university scene is serious, its student life is genuinely excellent, and its cost of living, while higher than many Spanish cities, is considerably lower than London, Dublin, or most major Australian cities. If your programme is in Madrid, this guide gives you the practical information you need: which university actually fits your subject, what things cost, where to live, and how the student visa works.

Universities in Madrid for International Students

Madrid has four major universities and several specialist institutions. They are not interchangeable — which one is right for you depends almost entirely on your subject area and your language of study.

Universidad Complutense de Madrid (UCM)

The Complutense is one of the oldest universities in the world and one of the largest in Europe, with around 80,000 students across dozens of faculties. Its main campus in Moncloa (Ciudad Universitaria) is a city within a city: multiple faculties, libraries, sports facilities, student residences, and its own metro stop. It is the right choice for students who want the breadth and prestige of a classic European research university, particularly in humanities, social sciences, law, medicine, and life sciences. It has a very large Erasmus incoming community and is well set up for non-Spanish students, though most undergraduate programmes are taught in Spanish. International students should expect a B2 or C1 level of Spanish to study effectively here.

Universidad Autónoma de Madrid (UAM)

The Autónoma is a smaller, research-intensive university with a strong reputation in natural sciences, biochemistry, philosophy, and humanities. Its campus is in Cantoblanco, north of the city — connected to the centre by Cercanías rail (around 30 minutes). It has a less central location than UCM but a more focused, campus-based environment. If you are interested in cutting-edge research, UAM consistently ranks alongside UCM in European research rankings and is in many areas the stronger research institution. It also hosts one of Spain's leading physics and mathematics faculties.

IE Business School

IE is Madrid's flagship private business school and one of the most prestigious in Europe, consistently ranked in the Financial Times top ten for MBA programmes. Its Salamanca campus is in the centre of the city. The vast majority of IE's programmes are taught in English, and its student body is deliberately international — around 130 nationalities in any given year. It is the right choice if you are pursuing a Master's in Business Administration, International Relations, Law, Architecture, or related fields, and you have the budget for private university fees (which are significantly higher than public university fees). IE's alumni network is genuinely global and career outcomes are strong.

Universidad Carlos III de Madrid

Carlos III is a public university with a strong reputation in law, economics, political science, and engineering. It has a significant offering of English-language undergraduate and postgraduate programmes — more than most Spanish public universities — which makes it particularly accessible to non-Spanish speakers. The main campus is in Leganés, south of the city (metro accessible). It consistently ranks among Spain's top universities for employability outcomes. A good option for students who want a rigorous, English-friendly public university experience.

ESNE (Escuela Universitaria de Diseño)

For students pursuing design, animation, user experience, or creative fields, ESNE is worth knowing. A private university of design based in Moncloa, it offers degrees in graphic design, animation, game design, and UX. Smaller than the major public universities, with a more applied, studio-based teaching model. Programmes are primarily in Spanish.

Spain Student Visa: Requirements and Documents

Non-EU students enrolled in a full-time programme of more than 90 days in Spain must apply for a Spain student visa at their local Spanish consulate before travelling. The core documents required:

  • Acceptance letter from your Madrid university or institution, confirming enrolment in a full-time programme
  • Proof of sufficient funds to support yourself for the duration of your studies — typically demonstrated via bank statement or a sponsor's financial guarantee. Spanish consulates generally look for approximately €600–€700 per month of study
  • Private health insurance valid in Spain, with no exclusions and no copayments
  • Clean criminal record certificate from your home country, apostilled (and translated into Spanish if issued in a language other than Spanish)
  • Medical certificate confirming you have no infectious diseases of public health significance
  • Completed national visa application form and recent passport photographs
  • Valid passport with at least one year's validity beyond your intended stay
  • Proof of paid tuition fees (or acceptance with fee payment plan confirmation)

Once in Spain, you must apply for your student TIE card (Tarjeta de Identidad de Extranjero) within one month of arrival. This is your legal residence document and proves your right to be in Spain as a student. The TIE application is made at the Oficina de Extranjería in Madrid.

Timing matters: Student visas should be applied for at least two to three months before your course start date. Spanish consulates in some countries have significant processing backlogs. Do not leave the visa application until the last minute.

Cost of Student Life in Madrid: Real Numbers

Madrid is a capital city and it is priced like one. It is cheaper than London, Dublin, or Amsterdam — but it is not as cheap as Salamanca, Granada, or Seville. Students who manage their spending carefully can live comfortably here; students who do not think about it can burn through money quickly. Here is what to realistically budget:

Accommodation options

Shared flat (piso compartido): This is how the vast majority of Madrid students live. A room in a shared flat in a student-adjacent neighbourhood typically costs €450–€700 per month. A central flat (Malasaña, Lavapiés, near UCM) will be at the higher end; outer districts like Carabanchel, Vallecas, or Arganzuela are at the lower end. Sharing with three or four flatmates brings costs down significantly.

University residencia (student hall): UCM and UAM have residencias on or near campus. These range from approximately €600–€900 per month for a single room with meals. Applications are competitive and must usually be made well in advance. The advantage is the ready-made social environment; the disadvantage is the cost relative to a shared flat and less flexibility.

Full monthly budget for a Madrid student

  • Rent (shared flat room, mid-range): €450–€650
  • Groceries and cooking at home: €180–€250
  • Eating out (café lunches, the occasional restaurant): €100–€180
  • Transport (student metro pass): €30–€55
  • Phone: €15–€25
  • Social spending, activities, going out: €100–€200
  • Health insurance and miscellaneous: €50–€80
  • Total: approximately €925–€1,440 per month

Tuition fees are additional and vary enormously: public university undergraduate fees at UCM and UAM start from approximately €800–€1,500 per academic year for EU students, but international (non-EU) student fees can be €3,000–€12,000+ depending on the programme. IE Business School MBA fees are in a different category entirely — typically €60,000–€80,000 for the full programme. Always confirm current tuition costs directly with your university.

Best Areas for Students in Madrid

Arganzuela and Carabanchel

South of the city centre, these districts offer the lowest rents in a well-connected part of Madrid. Arganzuela borders the Manzanares river park (a genuinely pleasant green space) and is connected to the rest of the city by multiple metro lines. Carabanchel is more residential and outer, with rents starting from €350–€450 for a room. Both areas are popular with students on tighter budgets and have improved significantly in quality of life over the past decade.

Lavapiés

Central, multicultural, and cheap by Madrid standards, Lavapiés is the neighbourhood of choice for many arts and humanities students at UCM. It has a vibrant independent culture — alternative cinemas, art spaces, independent theatres, cheap international food — and a very mixed, non-gentrified feel. It is not the tidiest neighbourhood in Madrid, but it has genuine character and cheap accommodation (rooms from €400 in shared flats).

Moncloa-Aravaca (Ciudad Universitaria)

For UCM students specifically, living in Moncloa — the district immediately around the campus — makes the most logistical sense. The area has plenty of student flats, good transport links (direct metro to city centre), and all the student infrastructure (cheap restaurants, copy shops, sports facilities) that campus life generates. Not the most exciting neighbourhood in Madrid, but extremely practical.

Malasaña and Chueca

More expensive (rooms €550–€750), but genuinely excellent places to live if you can afford them. The social life, café culture, and city energy in these central neighbourhoods are hard to beat and many students — particularly those on postgraduate programmes or with some family support — choose to pay a bit more to be at the heart of the city.

Part-Time Work on a Student Visa

International students on a Spain student visa are permitted to work part-time while studying. The current legal limit is 30 hours per week (updated from 20 hours under the 2022 reform), provided the work does not interfere with your studies.

Common jobs for international students in Madrid include English language teaching (demand is very high — a native English speaker with some preparation can find teaching work readily), work in bars, cafés, and restaurants (especially in tourist-adjacent areas), administrative or reception roles in international companies, and internships related to your field of study.

You must have your TIE student card before working legally. Working without documentation risks your visa status. Madrid's black-market labour market is real, but it is a significant legal risk for visa holders — stick to registered employment.

Getting to Madrid from the Airport

Madrid Barajas Airport (MAD) is one of Europe's largest airports. From the airport to the city centre, you have several options:

  • Metro Line 8 connects the airport directly to Nuevos Ministerios, from where you can transfer to other metro lines. Journey time approximately 25–35 minutes. Cost: standard metro fare plus a €3 supplement from the airport terminals
  • Cercanías Line C-1 connects Terminal 4 (T4) to Atocha and Chamartín stations. Useful if you are heading to the south or east of the city
  • Airport Express bus runs between the airport and Atocha station, 24 hours a day. Cost around €5. Useful if you have heavy luggage and want a direct bus
  • Taxi from any terminal to city centre: approximately €30–€35 fixed rate to most central destinations

For your first arrival with luggage, the Airport Express bus or taxi are the most straightforward options. Once you know the city, the metro is the daily standard.

Language Preparation

If your programme is in Spanish, your university will require proof of language proficiency (typically DELE B2 or equivalent) as part of your acceptance. But language proficiency for bureaucratic purposes and language proficiency for living comfortably in Madrid are two different things.

Daily life in Madrid functions in Spanish: going to the local authority offices to register your TIE, dealing with your landlord, navigating the healthcare system, making friends outside your international student bubble. Even if your classes are in English, a working level of spoken Spanish will meaningfully improve your experience of living in Madrid. The Spanish taught in standard language courses is entirely sufficient — Madrid uses standard Castilian Spanish, with a clear accent and no regional variations that would confuse a language learner.

If you arrive without much Spanish, Madrid has excellent language schools (the Official Language Schools — Escuelas Oficiales de Idiomas — are subsidised and excellent) and language exchange culture (intercambio) is widespread. Many Spanish students want to practice English in exchange for Spanish practice, and intercambio partners are easy to find via apps like Tandem or through university noticeboards.

Erasmus and International Programmes

Madrid universities are major Erasmus+ destinations. UCM and UAM receive thousands of Erasmus students each year — so many that the city has a well-established infrastructure for incoming exchange students: international student offices with English-speaking staff, buddy programmes connecting incoming students with Spanish peers, and a social calendar of events specifically for international students in the first weeks of term.

If you are arriving as an Erasmus student, your host university's international office is your first point of contact for housing lists, local registration guidance, and orientation events. The main intake is in September for the first semester and in January for the second.

For non-Erasmus international students — those arriving directly as full-degree students — the infrastructure is still good at the major universities, but you will need to be more proactive in the first weeks. Join the international student WhatsApp groups (usually organised through your faculty), attend orientation events, and use the ESN (Erasmus Student Network) events which are typically open to all international students.

Frequently Asked Questions

What visa do I need to study in Madrid?
Non-EU students enrolled in a full-time programme of more than 90 days in Spain need a Spain student visa (Visado de Estudios). This is applied for at your Spanish consulate before you travel. Once in Spain, you will also need to apply for a student TIE card, which functions as your legal residence document for the duration of your studies.
Which is the best university in Madrid for international students?
It depends on your subject. Universidad Complutense (UCM) is the largest — excellent for humanities, law, medicine, and sciences. UAM is stronger for research and natural sciences. IE Business School and Carlos III are the top choices for business and law taught in English. There is no single best university — the right choice is the one with the strongest programme in your specific field.
How much does it cost to live in Madrid as a student?
A realistic monthly budget for a student in Madrid is €925–€1,440. This covers your share of a shared flat (€450–€650), groceries and some eating out (€280–€430), transport (€30–€55 on a student metro pass), phone, and some social spending. Accommodation is the biggest variable — sharing with more people reduces costs significantly.
Can I work in Spain on a student visa?
Yes, with restrictions. Non-EU students in Spain are permitted to work up to 30 hours per week while studying, provided work does not interfere with your studies and you hold a valid TIE student card. Common student jobs include English language teaching, hospitality work, and internships. Working without documentation risks your visa status.
Do I need to speak Spanish to study in Madrid?
It depends on your programme. Many master's and MBA programmes at IE and Carlos III are taught entirely in English. Most undergraduate programmes at UCM and UAM are taught in Spanish and require B2 or C1 level. Even for English-taught programmes, functional Spanish significantly improves your day-to-day life in Madrid — bureaucracy, housing, and social integration all benefit from it.
What documents do I need for a Spain student visa?
Core documents include: university acceptance letter, proof of sufficient funds (approximately €600–€700 per month of study), private health insurance, apostilled criminal record certificate, medical certificate, passport photographs, and completed visa application form. Requirements can vary by consulate — always verify with the Spanish consulate in your country.

Living in Torrevieja as an Expat: The Real Costa Blanca

Torrevieja is not glamorous, not trendy, and not trying to be. It is a genuine coastal town with some of Spain's lowest living costs, extraordinary pink salt lakes, and an expat community so well established that English is a practical first language for daily life. Here's the honest guide.

Let's start with what Torrevieja is not. It is not Barcelona. It is not Malaga. It is not a city with a buzzing arts district, a startup ecosystem, or a restaurant scene that food writers come to profile. If you are looking for those things, this guide will point you elsewhere and you will be fine.

But if you are looking for a Mediterranean coastal town where you can live well on a modest income, surrounded by people who have already done exactly what you're planning, in weather that averages comfortably warm for most of the year, with an infrastructure built so thoroughly around English-speaking expats that your first months of settling in will be considerably easier than in almost any other Spanish town — then Torrevieja deserves your serious attention.

Located on the southern Costa Blanca in the province of Alicante, Torrevieja has a population of around 100,000 — a proper town, not a village, but not a city either. Estimates suggest that in some areas of the municipality, the foreign-born population approaches 40–50%. The British and Irish communities are among the largest and most established. Alongside them are significant Scandinavian, German, Belgian, and Eastern European communities. Many of these people have been here for twenty years or more. The infrastructure they have collectively built — the English-speaking doctors' surgeries, the estate agents, the social clubs, the local English-language newspaper — is not something that materialised overnight. It reflects decades of community building, and it is genuinely useful to new arrivals.

Las Salinas: Torrevieja's Most Extraordinary Feature

No guide to Torrevieja would be honest without opening a section on Las Salinas — because it is genuinely one of the most remarkable natural features on the Spanish coast, and most people outside the Costa Blanca have never heard of it.

Two salt lagoons flank the town: Laguna de Torrevieja and Laguna de La Mata. The Laguna de Torrevieja — the closer and larger of the two — turns extraordinary shades of pink, red, and violet depending on the season and the concentration of salt-loving microorganisms in the water. Flamingos are regular visitors. The scene looks, at its most striking, genuinely unreal — like something from the Atacama rather than southern Spain.

The salt produced here is distributed across Spain and much of Europe. The lagoon and its surrounding park are protected natural space, walkable from town, and a daily reminder that Torrevieja sits in an environment of unusual natural beauty. The salt and the microclimate it creates are also said to have therapeutic benefits for skin conditions and respiratory issues — this claim has been around long enough and is repeated frequently enough that it clearly has some basis in the experience of local residents, even if it is difficult to quantify precisely.

The Expat Community Infrastructure

This is where Torrevieja is genuinely unusual, and where the comparison to other expat-friendly towns in Spain breaks down in the town's favour. The English-speaking service infrastructure is not scattered, occasional, or tourist-focused. It is embedded into the fabric of daily life in a way that takes decades to develop and that you won't find at the same depth in many other Spanish towns of this size.

Healthcare

There are multiple private clinics in Torrevieja and the surrounding area with English-speaking doctors and nurses. The main public hospital — Hospital Universitario de Torrevieja, operated by the Ribera Salud group under a public-private partnership model — has a good reputation and covers the area's healthcare needs for serious medical issues. For routine healthcare, the English-speaking GP model in local private clinics is how most British expats manage day-to-day health, supplemented by private health insurance which is a requirement for non-EU residents on visa programmes.

Estate Agents

The property market in Torrevieja and its surrounding urbanisations is served by dozens of English-speaking estate agents, many of whom have been operating for fifteen or twenty years and understand the market intimately. Finding a rental or purchase property, understanding the legal process, and navigating Spanish property law with English-language assistance is genuinely straightforward here in a way it simply is not in less established expat areas.

Legal and Financial Services

English-speaking solicitors, tax advisers, and financial planners specifically serving the expat community operate in and around Torrevieja. For NLV applicants, DNV holders, and retirees managing cross-border pensions and tax obligations, this professional infrastructure is valuable and available.

Social Life

The social infrastructure is extensive. English-language clubs covering everything from golf, bowls, and bridge to choir, amateur dramatics, walking groups, and charitable organisations are active and well-established. If you are worried about social isolation as an expat, Torrevieja has a well-tested answer: there are more social groups here than you could join even if you tried. The question is more about which ones suit you than whether they exist.

Cost of Living in Torrevieja

Torrevieja is genuinely one of Spain's most affordable coastal towns. The comparison to Marbella, Malaga, or even Alicante city is stark.

  • One-bedroom apartment rental: €450–€750 per month
  • Two-bedroom apartment rental: €550–€950 per month
  • Groceries (Mercadona, Aldi, Lidl — all present): €200–€300 per month for a couple
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet): €100–€150 per month
  • Private health insurance (required for NLV holders): €80–€180 per month depending on age
  • Eating out (Torrevieja has good local restaurants, not just tourist-priced ones): €15–€25 for a meal for two with wine
  • Car ownership (many expats have a car): variable, but road tax and insurance are cheap in Spain

A couple on the Non-Lucrative Visa can live a full and comfortable life in Torrevieja for €1,400–€2,000 per month combined. Individual retirees managing carefully can live on less. This makes it one of the most financially accessible coastal Mediterranean locations available to non-EU residents.

Property: Buying in Torrevieja

Torrevieja and its surrounding urbanisations (Ciudad Quesada, Los Altos, Torreta Florida) offer some of the most affordable property prices on the Spanish coast. Two-bedroom apartments in reasonable condition can be found below €100,000–€120,000 in some areas. Three-bedroom villas with private pools exist in the surrounding urbanisations from around €180,000–€250,000.

These are not aspirational numbers in the context of Spanish coastal property — they are genuinely achievable for many British retirees selling a UK property and relocating. The trade-off is that these areas are not Marbella or the upmarket Costa del Sol — they are suburban Spanish coastal urbanisations with a heavy expat concentration, and the aesthetic is functional rather than picturesque. For buyers who prioritise value, space, and a pool over prestige postcode, the calculus is clear.

Getting Around

Torrevieja's town centre and beachfront promenade are flat and walkable. The beach is pleasant and accessible without a car. Within the town, many expats manage perfectly well on foot or by bicycle for day-to-day errands.

For trips beyond the town — and Torrevieja does not have the infrastructure of a large city — a car makes life significantly easier. There is no train station; the nearest regular rail connections are in Alicante, approximately 45 minutes by bus. Regular buses connect Torrevieja to Alicante city and to Murcia (around one hour). Alicante-Elche Airport (ALC) is 40–50 minutes by road, with excellent connections across Europe.

If you are an expat who has spent your working life in a major city and values easy access to big-city infrastructure, be honest with yourself about this before committing to Torrevieja. The town has excellent local amenities for its size, but it is not Alicante, and Alicante is not Madrid. For major medical procedures, specialist services, and international airport connections, you will be driving or taking a bus.

Day Trips and Wider Region

Torrevieja's position on the southern Costa Blanca gives good access to a varied surrounding region:

  • Alicante city: 45 minutes — shopping, nightlife, major services, ferry port
  • Cartagena: approximately one hour — a remarkable historic port city with Roman ruins and a good food scene
  • Murcia city: approximately one hour — a genuine inland Spanish city with good restaurants and a strong market culture
  • Benidorm and northern Costa Blanca: 1.5–2 hours — further away, but accessible for day trips
  • Valencia: approximately 2.5 hours by road — a major city worth periodic visits

Who Torrevieja Is Right For

Torrevieja makes most sense for expats who:

  • Prioritise low cost of living as a primary criterion
  • Want the reassurance of a very large, very established English-speaking community
  • Do not need major-city infrastructure on their doorstep
  • Value an active social life with organised group activities
  • Are happy with a smaller-town environment in exchange for Mediterranean warmth and affordability
  • Are considering buying property and want the most for their money on the Spanish coast

It makes less sense for expats who need regular access to major corporate, medical, or cultural infrastructure, or who want to be embedded in a cosmopolitan, internationally diverse urban environment. Those priorities point toward Malaga, Valencia, or Barcelona. Both types of expat are right — they just need different places.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Torrevieja a good place to live as an expat?
For the right person, yes. Torrevieja has one of the largest and most established British, Irish, Scandinavian, and German expat communities in Spain. The infrastructure for English-speaking residents is exceptionally well developed. Combined with some of Spain's lowest coastal living costs and a warm Mediterranean climate, it is a genuinely excellent choice for expats who prioritise affordability and community over big-city amenities.
How much does it cost to live in Torrevieja?
Torrevieja is among the most affordable coastal locations in Spain. A one-bedroom apartment typically rents for €450–€750 per month. A couple can live comfortably on €1,200–€1,600 per month all-in, covering rent, groceries, utilities, health insurance, and a social life. Property to buy is also among the cheapest on the Spanish coast.
What are Las Salinas de Torrevieja?
Las Salinas de Torrevieja are the famous pink salt lakes that flank the town — one of the Costa Blanca's most remarkable natural features. The lakes get their distinctive pink and red colour from microorganisms in the salt-rich water, and flamingos are regular visitors. The salt produced here supplies much of Spain and Europe. The lakes are also said to have therapeutic properties, and the surrounding park is walkable from the town centre.
Do I need to speak Spanish to live in Torrevieja?
Not to a great degree, though learning some Spanish always improves quality of life. Torrevieja has an unusually large proportion of English-speaking services — doctors, solicitors, estate agents, and many shops where staff speak English. English-language local newspapers are printed and distributed in the town. Many expats live very comfortably with only basic Spanish, though integrating into local Spanish life will reward language learning.
Is Torrevieja good for families?
Torrevieja can work well for families prioritising low living costs, outdoor lifestyle, and a safe environment. The main considerations are schooling (international school options may involve a commute) and big-city amenities (Alicante, 45 minutes away, is where you'll find major hospitals and a broader range of services). For families happy with a smaller-town environment, it is a strong option.
How do I get around Torrevieja without a car?
Torrevieja's town centre and beachfront are walkable and a bicycle is practical for most local errands. Regular buses connect to Alicante (around 45 minutes) and Murcia (around one hour). There is no train station in Torrevieja. For anything beyond the immediate town, a car makes life significantly easier — most expats in the area have one.

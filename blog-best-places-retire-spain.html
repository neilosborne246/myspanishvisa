Best Places to Retire in Spain in 2026

Sun, culture, affordable living, and world-class healthcare — Spain has long been one of the world's most sought-after retirement destinations. Here is where to seriously consider settling.

Home / Blog / Best Places to Retire in Spain in 2026

Spain consistently ranks among the top five retirement destinations in the world, and for good reason. With over 300 days of sunshine a year in the south, a public healthcare system ranked among Europe's finest, and a cost of living that makes pensions stretch considerably further than in the UK, US, or Canada, the appeal is straightforward. The harder question is not whether to retire in Spain — it is where.

The country spans dramatically different landscapes, climates, and cultures. The choice between sun-baked Andalusia, the breezy Mediterranean coast, a buzzing city like Valencia, or a quieter inland town involves real trade-offs. This guide covers the retirement destinations that consistently attract English-speaking expats, with honest assessments of what each offers and what it costs.

What Makes Spain Such a Strong Retirement Choice?

Before getting into specific locations, it helps to understand what makes Spain stand apart. Healthcare quality is a major factor: Spain's national health system is ranked seventh in the world by the World Health Organization, and private health insurance — which most non-EU retirees will hold as a visa requirement — is substantially cheaper than equivalent cover in the UK or US, typically running €80–€200 per month for a healthy retiree.

Food and daily living costs are another draw. A restaurant meal for two with wine rarely exceeds €35 outside tourist hotspots. Supermarket prices are around 30–40% lower than equivalent UK shops. Petrol, utility bills, and public transport are all meaningfully cheaper. A comfortable retired couple can live well in most Spanish cities on €2,000–€2,800 per month.

Spain also has well-established expat communities in dozens of towns, meaning English-speaking medical practices, social clubs, activity groups, and service providers are easy to find — particularly on the southern and eastern coasts.

Costa del Sol: The Classic Expat Retirement Belt

The Costa del Sol — the southern coastline of Malaga province — remains the heartland of British and Irish retirement in Spain. Towns like Fuengirola, Benalmadena, Torremolinos, Marbella, and Nerja have large established expat populations, excellent transport links via Malaga Airport, and an infrastructure that caters heavily to English speakers.

Malaga city itself has transformed over the past decade into a genuinely exciting urban destination — it now hosts the Pompidou Centre, the Picasso Museum, and a thriving restaurant scene, alongside its traditional beach lifestyle. Average rent for a one-bedroom apartment in the city sits around €900–€1,100 per month; in nearby towns it can fall to €700–€900.

Marbella sits at the higher end of the Costa del Sol, with luxury properties, international golf courses, and premium amenities — but prices reflect this. A one-bedroom in Marbella easily runs €1,200–€1,800 per month. For retirees on a comfortable but not lavish budget, towns like Fuengirola, Nerja, or Estepona offer more value while retaining the sunny coastal lifestyle.

The Costa del Sol suits retirees who want: an established English-speaking community, guaranteed sunshine (the area averages 320 sunny days per year), easy access to a major international airport, and a wide range of activities including golf, sailing, and hiking in the Andalusian mountains.

Costa Blanca: Value and Community on the East Coast

Alicante province's Costa Blanca — covering towns like Alicante city, Torrevieja, Javea, Denia, and Calpe — offers a slightly different proposition to the Costa del Sol. Prices are somewhat lower, the landscape is dramatic with limestone cliffs and turquoise coves, and the expat community is enormous, with hundreds of thousands of Northern Europeans having settled here over several decades.

Torrevieja is particularly notable for its high concentration of UK and Scandinavian retirees and its very affordable property and rental market — a one-bedroom apartment can be rented from as little as €550 per month. The town sits beside two large salt lakes, and the microclimate is warm and dry virtually year-round.

Javea (Xabia) offers a more scenically dramatic setting, sheltered by the Montgo mountain, with a mix of traditional Spanish town, beach resort, and hillside residential areas. It attracts a slightly more active expat demographic and has a strong range of hiking, cycling, and water sports alongside its established British and German communities.

Alicante city itself is often overlooked in favour of smaller coastal towns, but it offers a genuinely excellent quality of life: a beautiful old town, a castle overlooking the city, a long sandy beach right in the centre, good public transport, a major international airport, and living costs around 20–25% below Madrid or Barcelona.

Valencia: The Retiree Sweet Spot

Valencia has surged in popularity with retirees over the past five years and now represents perhaps the best overall balance of affordability, quality of life, and infrastructure in Spain. As Spain's third-largest city, it has all the amenities of a major metropolitan area — world-class hospitals, an efficient metro and tram network, an international airport — at a cost of living markedly lower than Madrid or Barcelona.

A one-bedroom apartment in a pleasant residential neighbourhood costs €800–€1,100 per month to rent. Buying in Valencia is also significantly more accessible than in the capital or Barcelona. The city has a growing English-speaking expat community, excellent private healthcare options, and a climate that delivers around 300 days of sunshine annually alongside mild winters.

Valencia's lifestyle is famously relaxed — it invented the concept of paella, after all — and the city sits within easy reach of the coast (the beach is just 4 km from the city centre), mountains, and the beautiful Albufera nature reserve. For retirees wanting a genuine Spanish city experience rather than a coastal expat enclave, Valencia is outstanding.

The Canary Islands: Year-Round Sunshine for UK Retirees

The Canary Islands — particularly Tenerife, Gran Canaria, and Lanzarote — occupy a special place in British retirement planning. Because of their subtropical latitude (off the coast of Morocco), the islands enjoy genuinely warm weather every month of the year, with temperatures rarely dropping below 18°C even in January. This makes them uniquely attractive for retirees whose primary concern is escaping cold northern winters.

Tenerife is the most popular choice, with huge established communities of British and German retirees in towns like Los Cristianos, Playa de las Americas, and Costa Adeje in the south, and a quieter expat presence around Puerto de la Cruz in the north. Rental costs are broadly comparable to the Costa Blanca — expect to pay €700–€1,100 for a one-bedroom apartment.

Gran Canaria's Las Palmas is increasingly attractive to retirees who want a real city alongside the subtropical climate — it has a large beach right in the city, a historic old town, and all the infrastructure of a significant Spanish city, at costs well below mainland tourist areas.

Mallorca: Island Retirement with a Premium Tag

The Balearic Islands, and Mallorca in particular, attract a wealthier international retirement demographic. Property prices are high, particularly in the south-west around Palma and the exclusive Port d'Andratx and Deià areas. But Mallorca offers something distinct: dramatic mountain and coastal scenery, a genuinely cosmopolitan atmosphere, excellent international schools and private hospitals, and direct flights to most UK and European cities year-round.

Palma itself is a beautiful city with a stunning Gothic cathedral, a thriving restaurant and bar scene, and a Passeig Maritim waterfront that rivals anything in mainland Spain. For retirees with a solid budget — €3,000+ per month — Mallorca offers an extremely high quality of life.

Madrid: City Retirement Done Properly

Retirement in a major capital city is not for everyone, but Madrid rewards those who choose it with unparalleled cultural richness, outstanding public transport (so a car is unnecessary), first-class healthcare including several world-renowned hospitals, and a social energy that keeps life interesting. The Salamanca, Chamartin, and Retiro districts attract affluent expat retirees; more affordable neighbourhoods like Vallecas or Carabanchel offer city living at lower cost.

Madrid's climate is a selling point often overlooked: the city sits at altitude and has warm, dry summers and crisp sunny winters — significantly more sunshine than northern Europe, though colder in December and January than the south. Rent in a decent central neighbourhood runs €1,200–€1,800 for a one-bedroom.

Which Visa Do Retirees Need?

EU and EEA citizens can move to Spain without a visa, simply registering as residents at their local town hall. For everyone else — UK nationals post-Brexit, Americans, Canadians, Australians, South Africans — a visa is required for stays longer than 90 days.

The Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) is the standard route for retirees. It allows you to live in Spain without working, provided you demonstrate sufficient passive income (pension, investments, rental income) — typically around €2,400 per month for a single applicant and €600 per additional family member. You will also need private health insurance, a clean criminal record, and a medical certificate.

The NLV is initially issued for one year and can be renewed for two-year periods. After five years of continuous legal residency you can apply for long-term residency, and after ten years for Spanish citizenship (timelines vary by nationality). Frequently Asked Questions

What visa do non-EU retirees need to live in Spain?
Non-EU retirees typically apply for Spain's Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV). It allows you to live in Spain without working, provided you meet the minimum passive income threshold — around €2,400 per month for a single applicant in 2026. It is initially granted for one year and is renewable.
How much money do you need to retire in Spain?
Your monthly budget depends heavily on where you live. In smaller inland cities you can live well on €1,500–€2,000 per month. In popular coastal areas like Marbella or Palma de Mallorca, budget €2,500–€3,500 per month or more for a comfortable lifestyle. The Non-Lucrative Visa requires you to demonstrate passive income of at least €2,400 per month for a single applicant.
Is Spain a good country to retire to?
Spain is consistently rated among the world's top retirement destinations. It offers a world-class healthcare system, a Mediterranean diet and lifestyle, a warm climate in most regions, an affordable cost of living compared to the UK or US, and large established expat communities in cities like Malaga, Valencia, and Alicante.
Where do most British retirees live in Spain?
British retirees are most heavily concentrated on the Costa del Sol (Malaga, Marbella, Fuengirola, Nerja) and the Costa Blanca (Alicante, Torrevieja, Javea, Denia). The Canary Islands — particularly Tenerife and Gran Canaria — are also extremely popular with UK retirees seeking year-round warmth.
Do I need to learn Spanish to retire in Spain?
You can live comfortably in popular expat areas with little Spanish, as English is widely spoken in tourist zones, private healthcare settings, and expat services. However, learning basic conversational Spanish will significantly improve your daily life, help you integrate into your community, and is essential for dealing with local bureaucracy.
Can I access Spanish healthcare as a retiree on the Non-Lucrative Visa?
NLV holders are required to have private health insurance as a condition of their visa. UK retirees with an S1 form from the UK government may be able to access Spanish public healthcare. Once you have held legal residency for a set period and become a tax resident, you may eventually access the public system through the Convenio Especial arrangement.

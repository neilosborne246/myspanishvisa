Retiring in Malaga: Complete Expat Guide for 2026

Malaga offers everything a retiree could want from Spanish city life — 320 days of sunshine, world-class culture, an international airport, and one of the largest British expat communities in Europe.

Home / Blog / Retiring in Malaga: Complete Expat Guide for 2026

Malaga has undergone a remarkable transformation in the past decade. Once seen primarily as a transit point — people flew through on their way to beach resorts like Marbella and Torremolinos — the city has established itself as one of the finest destinations in southern Europe in its own right. The Picasso Museum, the Pompidou Centre, the renovated port and waterfront, and a restaurant scene that has genuinely earned international recognition have all contributed to a city that now attracts retirees who want culture and urban life alongside Mediterranean sunshine.

For non-EU retirees — British nationals post-Brexit, Americans, Canadians, Australians, and South Africans — Malaga represents an appealing combination: the infrastructure and connectivity of a major city (590,000 people in the city itself, over a million in the metro area), the warmest winters of any mainland Spanish city, and a support infrastructure for English speakers that is simply not available in smaller, cheaper towns.

Why Retirees Choose Malaga

The climate is usually the first thing mentioned, and it genuinely deserves the attention. Malaga averages 320 sunny days per year — the highest of any mainland Spanish city. January temperatures average around 17°C during the day, meaning outdoor cafe culture, beach walks, and alfresco dining continue year-round. Even the peak summer heat (mid-30s in July and August) is tempered by the sea breeze that comes off the Mediterranean. There is no frost, no snow, and no grey months.

But climate is not the full picture. Malaga Airport (Aeropuerto Internacional de Malaga-Costa del Sol) is one of Spain's busiest international airports, with direct flights to dozens of UK destinations, US east coast cities, Canada, and Australia via major hubs. For retirees who want to maintain frequent contact with family at home, the direct connectivity is a significant practical advantage over smaller cities.

The city's cultural offer has expanded dramatically. The Museo Picasso Malaga (Pablo Picasso was born here) houses over 200 works. The Centre Pompidou Malaga — a southern European outpost of the Paris original — brings contemporary art exhibitions. The Museo de Arte Flamenco documents Andalusia's most distinctive cultural tradition. There are more than 30 museums in total, a thriving theatre and music scene, and a film festival of international standing.

Cost of Living in Malaga

Malaga is no longer as cheap as it was a decade ago — the combination of its rising profile, growth in remote workers, and international property investment has pushed prices up. But it remains considerably more affordable than equivalent cities in the UK, France, or Italy.

Rental costs in 2026 vary significantly by area:

  • City centre / Centro Historico: €1,000–€1,400 per month for a one-bedroom apartment. The historic streets and pedestrianised areas make this highly walkable, though rents have risen with demand.
  • Soho and port area: €950–€1,300 per month. The renovated arts and marina district attracts younger professionals and retirees who want a modern city feel close to the sea.
  • Huelin: €800–€1,050 per month. A solid middle-class residential neighbourhood 2km west of the centre, with good supermarkets, cafes, and a beach promenade nearby.
  • El Palo and Pedregalejo: €750–€1,000 per month. East of the centre, these traditional fishing villages-turned-residential neighbourhoods have a genuine local feel and excellent chiringuito beach bars.

Beyond rent, a comfortable retired couple in Malaga can expect to spend:

  • Groceries: €350–€500 per month
  • Dining out (several times per week): €250–€400 per month
  • Private health insurance: €160–€300 per month for a couple
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet): €150–€200 per month
  • Transport (public or occasional taxi): €50–€100 per month

Total for a comfortable retired couple: approximately €2,800–€4,000 per month, depending heavily on rental situation and lifestyle choices.

Healthcare in Malaga

Malaga's healthcare provision is one of its strongest selling points for retirees. The public hospital network — anchored by the Hospital Regional Universitario de Malaga and the Hospital Clinico Universitario Virgen de la Victoria — provides comprehensive care of a very high standard. However, most non-EU retirees on the Non-Lucrative Visa will access private healthcare, which in Malaga is excellent and notably more affordable than equivalent private care in the UK or US.

Private hospitals including Hospital Quironsalud Malaga, HC Marbella (within easy reach), and a network of private clinics throughout the city offer English-speaking doctors and specialists, short waiting times, and high-quality diagnostic and surgical facilities. A comprehensive private health insurance policy for a healthy retiree in their 60s typically runs €80–€160 per month — a fraction of equivalent US premiums.

UK nationals who receive an S1 form from the UK government before moving may be able to access Spanish public healthcare directly. The S1 is issued to those who have reached UK state pension age and are receiving a UK state pension — it allows Spain to reclaim the costs of healthcare from the UK. If you think you may be eligible, check with the UK's NHSBSA before your move.

Best Neighbourhoods for Expat Retirees

Centro Historico

For those who want to be in the heart of it all, the historic centre delivers. Everything is walkable — the museums, the markets, the cafes on Calle Larios, the cathedral, the port. The streets are pedestrianised and beautifully maintained. It is busy with tourists in summer, but the locals who live here do not seem to mind — and the social energy it creates is part of the appeal for many retirees.

El Palo and Pedregalejo

These two adjoining neighbourhoods on Malaga's eastern seafront are highly popular with retiring expats who want a more village-like atmosphere within the city. The Paseo Maritimo runs along the beach, with restaurants, chiringuitos (beach bars), and a genuinely local population. El Palo has a strong expat presence, English-speaking services, and a very walkable layout.

Huelin

A largely residential neighbourhood on the western side of the centre, Huelin combines good value (lower rents than the historic centre), excellent supermarket access, a nearby beach promenade, and convenient bus routes. It has less of a tourist feel than the centre and suits retirees who want to live more like a local.

Towns Near Malaga

For retirees who want more space, a garden, or lower property prices, the towns and villages of Malaga province offer excellent alternatives within easy reach of the city:

  • Torremolinos: 12km west, established British expat community, beaches, good services, very affordable rents (€650–€900 for a one-bedroom)
  • Fuengirola: 30km west, extensive beach, large expat population, strong English-speaking healthcare and services
  • Nerja: 50km east, charming smaller town, beautiful Balcon de Europa viewpoint, more peaceful than the western Costa del Sol
  • Alhaurin de la Torre: 15km inland, golf courses, larger properties at lower prices, still well-connected to Malaga city
  • Marbella: 50km west, premium lifestyle, golf, marina, luxury amenities — at substantially higher prices

Getting Around Malaga

Malaga is one of Spain's most walkable cities for daily life, and this is one of the features retirees consistently mention. The flat, pedestrianised city centre means that most daily errands — the market, pharmacies, cafes, restaurants, the park — can be completed on foot.

Beyond walking, Malaga has an excellent public transport network. The city bus (EMT) covers the city and surrounding municipalities thoroughly. The suburban Cercanias train line runs west along the coast through Torremolinos, Benalmadena, Fuengirola — extremely useful if you want day-trip access to the wider Costa del Sol without a car. A new metro line opened in 2014 and has since been extended. Taxis and ride-hailing services are readily available and inexpensive.

For those who do want to drive, parking in the city centre is challenging (and expensive), but the wider city and suburban areas are car-friendly. The AP-7 motorway runs the length of the Costa del Sol, giving easy access to Marbella, Estepona, and beyond.

What Visa Do Malaga Retirees Need?

EU and EEA citizens can move to Malaga without a visa — they simply register at the town hall and obtain an NIE number (foreigner's identification number) as residents. For everyone else, a visa is required for stays beyond 90 days in any 180-day period.

The Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) is the standard route for non-EU retirees. It allows you to live in Spain without working, provided you demonstrate sufficient passive income. The 2026 monthly income threshold for a single applicant is approximately €2,400; for a couple, approximately €3,000 (€2,400 + €600 for each additional dependent). You also need private health insurance, a criminal record certificate from your home country, and a medical certificate.

The visa is initially granted for one year, then renewable in two-year blocks. After five years of continuous legal residency you can apply for long-term EU residency. After ten years (five for some nationalities with special treaties with Spain) you can apply for Spanish citizenship.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much money do you need to retire in Malaga?
A single retiree can live comfortably in Malaga on €1,800–€2,500 per month, depending on their lifestyle and neighbourhood. This covers rent (€900–€1,300 for a one-bedroom), food (€300–€400), private health insurance (€80–€160), utilities (€80–€120), and discretionary spending. The Non-Lucrative Visa requires demonstrating approximately €2,400 per month in passive income.
Is Malaga a good place to retire for British expats?
Yes — Malaga is one of the most popular retirement destinations for British nationals in Spain. The city and surrounding Costa del Sol have one of the largest established British expat communities in Europe, with English-speaking doctors, lawyers, social clubs, and services of all kinds. The climate, with 320 days of sunshine and mild winters, is a major draw for those escaping the UK's grey skies.
What is the best neighbourhood to retire in Malaga?
For central living with walkability, the Soho and Centro Historico areas are excellent. For a relaxed beach neighbourhood feel, El Palo and Pedregalejo are popular with retirees. Huelin is a well-connected, affordable residential area 2km west of the centre. For those wanting more space on a lower budget, Alhaurin de la Torre and other inland towns within Malaga province offer very competitive prices.
What visa do British retirees need to live in Malaga?
Post-Brexit, British nationals are treated as non-EU citizens and need a visa to stay in Spain longer than 90 days. The Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) is the standard route for retirees — it permits residence without working, provided you meet the income threshold (approximately €2,400 per month) and hold private health insurance. UK nationals with an S1 form may have different healthcare access arrangements.
What is the healthcare like in Malaga for expats?
Malaga has excellent healthcare infrastructure, including the large Hospital Regional Universitario de Malaga and Hospital Clinico Universitario Virgen de la Victoria on the public side, plus numerous well-equipped private hospitals and clinics popular with expats, such as Hospital Quironsalud and HC Marbella. Private health insurance costs €80–€160 per month for a healthy retiree and gives fast access to English-speaking specialists.
Is Malaga getting too expensive for retirees?
Malaga's property and rental market has risen significantly over the past five years. It is no longer among Spain's cheapest cities. However, it remains considerably more affordable than equivalent destinations in the UK, France, or Italy. Retirees on tighter budgets can consider nearby towns like Torremolinos, Fuengirola, or inland Alhaurin de la Torre for better value while remaining close to Malaga's amenities.

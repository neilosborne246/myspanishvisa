Starting a Business in Spain as a Foreigner: Step-by-Step Guide (2026)

Spain is an increasingly attractive location for entrepreneurs and remote workers. Whether you are launching a new venture or relocating an existing business, here is what you need to know about the legal structures, visa requirements, and practical steps involved.

Spain has quietly become one of Europe's most appealing destinations for international entrepreneurs. A lower cost of living than most of northern Europe, a strategic location, a large domestic market, and improving startup infrastructure make it an attractive base. Add in the lifestyle — the climate, the food, the pace of life — and it is not surprising that more and more English-speaking expats are choosing Spain not just to live, but to build their businesses.

But starting a business in Spain as a foreign national involves navigating a bureaucratic system that is quite different from what most English speakers are used to. This guide gives you a clear picture of the key decisions and steps involved.

First Question: Do You Need a Visa to Work in Spain?

Your residency status determines everything else. It is the foundation on which all other decisions rest.

EU / EEA Citizens

If you hold citizenship in an EU or EEA country, you have the right to live, work, and operate a business in Spain without needing a visa or work permit. You simply need to register as a resident and obtain your NIE number, then follow the same registration process as Spanish nationals.

Non-EU Citizens (UK, US, Canada, Australia, South Africa, etc.)

If you hold a passport from outside the EU/EEA, you need the right visa before you can legally live in Spain and operate a business there. The most relevant options are:

  • Digital Nomad Visa (DNV): Ideal for freelancers, remote workers, and online business owners. Requires you to work primarily for clients outside Spain (at least 80% of income from non-Spanish sources) and to demonstrate a minimum monthly income. The DNV is now well-established and our most common visa for business-minded expats.
  • Entrepreneur Visa (Visa Emprendedores): Designed for non-EU nationals launching an innovative or high-impact business in Spain. Requires a favourable report from Spain's Economic and Commercial Office. More complex to obtain than the DNV but allows you to operate a Spanish-focused business from day one.
  • Self-Employment Visa (Cuenta Propia): The traditional route for non-EU citizens who want to work as autónomo in Spain. Requires detailed documentation including a business plan, proof of professional qualifications, and evidence of sufficient financial resources.

Important: Working or operating a business in Spain without the right visa or work permit is illegal and can result in fines, deportation, and a ban on re-entering Spain. Always get your residency status sorted before you start trading.

Choosing the Right Legal Structure for Your Spanish Business

Spain has several business structures available to foreign entrepreneurs. The right choice depends on your income level, risk exposure, number of partners, and plans for growth.

Autónomo (Self-Employed Sole Trader)

The autónomo structure is the simplest way to operate a business in Spain. Think of it as equivalent to being a sole trader in the UK or a sole proprietor in the US. There is no minimum capital requirement, registration is relatively straightforward, and you can start operating quickly.

The main drawback: you are personally liable for all business debts. If the business owes money, creditors can pursue your personal assets. This is fine for low-risk freelancers and consultants, but more risky for businesses with significant liabilities.

As an autónomo, you pay income tax (IRPF) on your profits and make monthly social security contributions. The monthly social security rate is on a sliding scale based on net profits, starting at approximately €230 per month for those with lower incomes.

Sociedad Limitada — SL (Limited Liability Company)

The SL is the Spanish equivalent of a UK limited company or a US LLC. It is the most popular structure for small to medium-sized businesses because it limits personal liability to the capital invested. Shareholders cannot be pursued for debts beyond their capital contribution.

An SL requires a minimum share capital of €3,000 (which can be gradually paid up under certain rules). Setting one up involves registering the company name, signing the articles of association before a Notary, and registering with the Commercial Registry. Total setup costs including professional fees typically run to €1,500–€3,000.

An SL pays corporate income tax (Impuesto de Sociedades) at 25% on profits (new companies may benefit from a reduced 15% rate in their first two profitable years).

Sociedad Anónima — SA (Public Limited Company)

The SA is for larger businesses requiring substantial capital and formal governance structures. It requires a minimum capital of €60,000 and involves more complex administration. Most foreign entrepreneurs launching small or medium businesses will not need this structure.

Step-by-Step: How to Register a Business in Spain

  • Step 1 — Obtain your NIE number. Your Número de Identidad de Extranjero is required for all official processes. Apply through the Spanish consulate in your home country before you arrive, or at a Spanish police station once you are in Spain.
  • Step 2 — Open a Spanish bank account. Required for paying business taxes, receiving client payments, and depositing the share capital for an SL.
  • Step 3 — Choose and register your business name. For an SL, check that the company name is available through the Central Mercantile Registry (Registro Mercantil Central) and obtain a certificate of name uniqueness.
  • Step 4 — Sign the Articles of Association before a Notary. For an SL, the founders must appear before a Notary Public to sign the constituting documents and deposit the share capital.
  • Step 5 — Register with the Tax Agency (Agencia Tributaria). Apply for your company's Tax Identification Number (CIF) and register for the appropriate taxes (VAT — IVA, and corporate income tax or IRPF for autónomos).
  • Step 6 — Register with Social Security (Seguridad Social). As a business owner operating in Spain, you will typically need to register as autónomo with the social security system, even if operating through an SL.
  • Step 7 — Obtain any licences or permits required for your activity. Some business activities (food service, healthcare, construction, childcare) require specific local or national licences. Your gestor or lawyer can advise what applies to your sector.

The Gestor: Your Most Important Local Ally

A gestor is a Spanish administrative professional who specialises in tax filings, social security management, and bureaucratic compliance. Almost every business in Spain — large and small — works with a gestor. For foreign entrepreneurs who are not fluent in Spanish and unfamiliar with the Spanish fiscal system, a good English-speaking gestor is genuinely invaluable.

In Spain, quarterly VAT returns, annual income tax filings, social security payments, and various other declarations have strict deadlines. Missing them triggers automatic fines. A gestor handles all of this, typically for a monthly fee of €60–€150 for a straightforward autónomo setup, or more for companies with employees or complex accounting needs.

Taxes You Need to Know About

Operating a business in Spain means engaging with the Spanish tax system. The key taxes for business owners are:

  • IRPF (Income Tax): Autónomos pay income tax on their profits. Spain uses a progressive rate, rising from 19% to 47% depending on your income level and region.
  • IVA (VAT): The standard Spanish VAT rate is 21%, with reduced rates of 10% and 4% for certain goods and services. Most businesses must file quarterly IVA returns.
  • Impuesto de Sociedades (Corporate Tax): SL companies pay 25% on profits (15% for qualifying new companies in their first two profitable years).
  • Social Security Contributions: Self-employed individuals registered as autónomo pay monthly contributions to the Seguridad Social, which covers healthcare, pension rights, and sickness benefits.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I start a business in Spain as a non-resident?
Yes, you can register a company in Spain as a non-resident. However, you will need a Spanish NIE number, a Spanish bank account, and a Spanish address for the registered office. Non-EU citizens who also want to live in Spain will need to obtain the appropriate visa before they can be resident directors or work within the business.
What is an autónomo and is it right for me?
An autónomo is a self-employed sole trader. It is the simplest and cheapest way to operate a business in Spain, with no minimum capital required and straightforward registration. You are personally liable for business debts, so it works well for freelancers, consultants, and sole traders with low financial risk. For businesses with significant assets or multiple partners, a Sociedad Limitada (SL) offers better protection.
What visa do I need to run a business in Spain?
Non-EU citizens who want to live in Spain and actively run a business there need a work permit or a suitable visa. The Digital Nomad Visa is a strong option for remote workers and online business owners. The Entrepreneur Visa (Emprendedores) is designed for those launching innovative businesses in Spain. EU citizens have the right to establish and run a business in Spain without a separate visa.
How much does it cost to set up an SL company in Spain?
Setting up a Sociedad Limitada (SL) requires a minimum share capital of €3,000 (under standard rules). Additional costs include notary fees (typically €500–€800), company name registration, and administrative costs. Total setup costs, including professional fees, typically range from €1,500 to €3,000. Ongoing costs include an accountant (gestor), annual accounts filing, and social security contributions if you are registered as autónomo through the company.
Do I need a gestor to run a business in Spain?
Not legally, but practically speaking, almost all businesses in Spain use a gestor — a Spanish administrative professional who handles tax filings, social security payments, and bureaucratic requirements. Spanish tax law and business administration are complex, and the penalties for errors or late filing are significant. A good gestor is one of the best investments you can make when starting a business in Spain.

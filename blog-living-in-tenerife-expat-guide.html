Living in Tenerife as an Expat: What Nobody Tells You

Tenerife is Spain's most populated island and one of Europe's most popular expat destinations — but the Tenerife in the brochures and the Tenerife you actually live in can be quite different. Here is the honest guide.

Tenerife is Spain's most populated island — home to around 900,000 people — and one of Europe's most consistently popular expat destinations. Tens of thousands of British, German, Irish, and Scandinavian expats have made it their permanent home. The appeal is easy to understand: year-round warmth, relatively low cost of living, excellent flight connections, and a long-established English-speaking infrastructure that makes the practical side of moving abroad considerably easier than almost anywhere else.

But Tenerife is not a single, uniform place. It is, in a very real sense, two distinct islands in one. The sunny, Anglophone resort south — Los Cristianos, Playa de las Américas, Costa Adeje — and the greener, quieter, more authentically Spanish north — Puerto de la Cruz, La Orotava, La Laguna — offer genuinely different ways of life. Choosing between them is not a minor detail. It is the most important decision you will make before you arrive.

The North-South Divide — Which Tenerife Do You Want?

The island's character splits almost completely along geographic lines, shaped largely by the Teide volcano (3,718 metres — Spain's highest peak) that dominates the centre and creates remarkable landscape and climate diversity across a relatively small landmass.

The south — Los Cristianos, Playa de las Américas, Costa Adeje — is the resort strip. Guaranteed sunshine through the year. A massive English-speaking infrastructure built over decades of British and Irish tourism. British supermarkets stocking your familiar brands. Irish pubs showing Premier League football. An expat community so large and well-established that you can spend weeks without needing to speak a word of Spanish. This suits a certain kind of expat perfectly — particularly retirees who want comfort, familiarity, and a ready-made social scene from day one.

The north — Puerto de la Cruz, La Orotava, La Laguna — is a different proposition. Cooler temperatures, markedly greener vegetation, more genuine Spanish character. Puerto de la Cruz is an older, more traditional town popular with over-60s who want authenticity rather than resort convenience. The architecture is more interesting, the pace is slower, the community is more integrated into Spanish life. You will need more Spanish here. The cost is lower.

Both sides of the island are valid choices. The mistake is picking the one that looks better in a photograph rather than the one that matches your actual personality and way of life.

Cost of Living in Tenerife — Real Numbers for 2026

Tenerife is cheaper than mainland Spain on average, and considerably cheaper than the UK, Ireland, or Australia. One significant structural advantage: the Canary Islands operate under their own tax regime, with IGIC (Impuesto General Indirecto Canario) set at 7% rather than the mainland VAT rate of 21%. This keeps the cost of goods, services, and dining noticeably lower than equivalent mainland Spanish cities.

Rent

  • 1-bedroom apartment in Puerto de la Cruz (north): €650–€850/month
  • 1-bedroom apartment in Costa Adeje area (south): €750–€1,000/month
  • 1-bedroom apartment in Santa Cruz de Tenerife (capital): €800–€1,100/month

Other monthly costs (single person)

  • Groceries: €200–€280/month
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet): €80–€120/month — mild climate keeps bills lower than mainland
  • Eating out: menú del día in local bars €10–€12; tourist-zone restaurants cost significantly more

Total comfortable monthly budget

  • North Tenerife (Puerto de la Cruz): approximately €1,400–€1,900/month
  • South Tenerife (resort areas): approximately €1,600–€2,200/month

Both figures are substantially lower than what you would spend for comparable quality of life in London, Dublin, Sydney, or Toronto. Even compared to mainland Spanish cities like Madrid or Barcelona, Tenerife represents meaningfully better value.

Best Areas to Live in Tenerife as an Expat

Los Cristianos

The oldest and most established of the south resort towns. Los Cristianos has a larger permanent expat population than the more tourist-transient Playa de las Américas next door. Good facilities, a working harbour, a real beach, walking groups, social clubs. Better value than Costa Adeje. This is where many long-term British residents end up.

Costa Adeje

The upmarket southern option. Newer, more manicured developments, a better standard of restaurant, marina facilities, golf courses nearby. Popular with retirees who want comfort and resort-standard infrastructure. More expensive than Los Cristianos, but the quality of amenities justifies it for many.

Playa de las Américas

Hectic, noisy, tourist-heavy — genuinely fun to visit but challenging as a permanent base for most expats. The nightlife infrastructure is substantial; the settled, community feel is limited. Most permanent residents in the south migrate towards Los Cristianos or Costa Adeje for day-to-day living.

Puerto de la Cruz (north)

The clear choice for expats who want real Tenerife rather than resort Tenerife. An older town with a beautiful historic centre, lush vegetation on the hillsides above, cooler temperatures, and genuine Spanish cultural life. Lower costs than the south. A strong over-60s expat community with deep roots in the island. More Spanish is needed here, but the cultural richness on offer makes the investment worthwhile for many.

La Laguna and Santa Cruz de Tenerife

La Laguna is a UNESCO-listed university town with genuine Spanish character, popular with those working or with families. Santa Cruz de Tenerife, the island's capital, offers a proper city experience — larger shops, more administrative services, a city rhythm — without the tourist overlay of the south. Both require more Spanish and offer less of the expat-community infrastructure found in the resort zones.

Healthcare in Tenerife

Spain's public healthcare system is world-class, but Non-Lucrative Visa holders are required to hold private health insurance as a visa condition. This is not optional — it is a stated requirement for your permit. The good news is that private healthcare in Tenerife is genuinely good and considerably more affordable than equivalent coverage at home.

In the south, Hospiten and Vithas are the major private hospital chains, with facilities across the resort areas and English-speaking staff throughout. Hospital Universitario de Canarias in La Laguna serves serious or complex cases and functions as the main public hospital for the north. Private health insurance for most expats costs approximately €100–€200 per month depending on age and the level of coverage selected.

British residents who receive a UK State Pension may be able to register an S1 form, which allows access to Spanish public healthcare at UK expense. This is worth exploring with a specialist before you apply for your visa, as it can significantly reduce or eliminate the need for private cover.

Pharmacies are everywhere on the island — Tenerife has excellent pharmaceutical access, and many common medications are available without prescription or at very low cost.

Weather Year-Round — The Main Draw

The "eternal spring" description of Tenerife is real, but it is not uniform across the island. Understanding the difference matters before you decide where to live.

The south, sheltered by the Teide massif, receives very little rainfall and delivers consistently warm, sunny weather throughout the year: 22–27°C in summer, 18–22°C in winter. Almost no overcast days. This is the climate that made the resort strip what it is — and it is the single biggest pull for northern European retirees and expats who have spent too many years under grey skies.

The north is cooler and more variable. Summer temperatures of 18–23°C give way to 14–18°C in winter, with more cloud cover and genuine rainfall — which is precisely why the north is so dramatically green compared to the near-desert south. The lushness comes at a cost. If guaranteed sunshine is non-negotiable for you, the north will occasionally disappoint.

The key point about winter in the south: even in January, you can sit outside for lunch in a t-shirt. This is not an exaggeration. It is the reason so many northern European retirees choose Tenerife over mainland Spain, where winters — even in Andalusia — can be noticeably cooler and damper.

Getting Around — You Will Need a Car

Outside Santa Cruz and the resort strip (where taxis, buses, and walking cover most needs), a car is essentially mandatory for comfortable expat life in Tenerife. The TITSA public bus network covers the island but operates on slow, infrequent schedules outside the main tourist corridors. If you want to explore the interior, reach the north from the south easily, or access services that are not within walking distance of your front door, a car is the practical reality.

Budget approximately €150–€250 per month once you factor in insurance, fuel, and running costs. Roads are generally good. The TF-1 motorway runs north-south along the east coast, connecting the airport and resort areas to Santa Cruz and providing reasonable access to the north. The mountain roads leading up towards Teide and across to the west of the island are excellent driving but require care.

Language — How Much Spanish Do You Actually Need?

The honest answer depends entirely on where you live. The south runs substantially in English. Staff in shops, restaurants, government offices in the resort zone, and most service businesses speak English as standard. You can function entirely in English in Costa Adeje or Los Cristianos. Many expats do, for years, without difficulty.

The north is a different story. Puerto de la Cruz, La Laguna, and the interior require meaningful Spanish for daily life. Administrative processes — dealing with the ayuntamiento, setting up utilities, registering with a doctor — require Spanish everywhere on the island, regardless of how English-friendly your immediate area feels.

Regardless of where you settle, some Spanish is always valuable — and local Canarians genuinely appreciate the effort. It is worth noting that Canarian Spanish has its own distinctive accent, pace, and some local vocabulary that differs from mainland Spanish. Arriving with Castilian Spanish will serve you fine; arriving with no Spanish at all will limit your world, particularly outside the resort zones.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Tenerife expensive to live in?
Tenerife is cheaper than mainland Spain and far cheaper than the UK, Ireland, or Australia. Budget approximately €1,400–€1,900 per month for a comfortable single-person life in the north, or €1,600–€2,200 in the south resort areas. The Canary Islands benefit from a lower local tax (IGIC at 7% rather than mainland VAT at 21%), which keeps everyday costs noticeably down.
Which is better — north or south Tenerife for expats?
It depends entirely on personality. The south offers guaranteed sunshine, English-speaking infrastructure, and a resort feel that makes settling in very easy. The north is greener, cooler, more authentically Spanish, and lower cost. Most retirees choose the south; most long-term expats who want cultural integration tend to lean north. Neither is wrong — they are genuinely different islands in one.
Do I need a car in Tenerife?
Yes, unless you are based entirely in Santa Cruz or a resort strip and don't plan to explore beyond it. The TITSA bus network exists but is slow and limited. A car is the practical reality for most expats, costing around €150–€250 per month once you factor in insurance and fuel.
What is the weather like in winter in Tenerife?
The south typically sees 18–22°C in January — warm enough to sit outside for lunch. Prolonged cold periods are essentially unknown. The north is cooler and wetter in winter, averaging 14–18°C, but still extremely mild by northern European standards. Even on overcast days in the north, temperatures remain comfortable.
Is healthcare good in Tenerife?
Yes. Private hospital networks Hospiten and Vithas operate throughout the island, particularly in the south. Hospital Universitario de Canarias in La Laguna handles complex cases for the north. Private health insurance — required as a condition of the Non-Lucrative Visa — is affordable and comprehensive. Most expats are satisfied with the standard of private care, which is substantially cheaper than equivalent coverage in the UK or Australia.
What visa do I need to live in Tenerife?
Non-EU citizens — British, American, Canadian, Australian, and others — typically apply for Spain's Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) if retiring or not working, or the Digital Nomad Visa (DNV) if working remotely for a non-Spanish employer. Both require applying through your Spanish consulate before you arrive. Tenerife, as part of Spain, follows the same national visa rules as the mainland.

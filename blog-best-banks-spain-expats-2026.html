Best Banks in Spain for Expats 2026: An Honest Comparison

Every expat forum has a banking thread. Someone asks which bank is best, twelve people mention their bank, someone brings up Revolut, it turns into an argument about fees. Here's the actual answer — based on what works in practice for foreigners living in Spain.

Updated July 2026
7 banks compared
Expat-focused guidance
Resident & non-resident accounts

Opening a bank account in Spain is one of the first things on everyone's list when they move here — and with good reason. You'll need it for your rental deposit, for setting up utilities, for paying into the Spanish social security system if you're working, and for a hundred other things that require a local account rather than a foreign card you're hoping will work.

The question is: which bank? Spain has several major banks, a handful of digital-only options, and a growing number of international fintechs that work fine in Spain but aren't quite the same as having a proper Spanish account. We've seen what works smoothly (and what causes headaches) for expats going through the NLV, DNV, and student visa processes. Here's the breakdown.

This article focuses on comparison — which bank and why. If you want step-by-step guidance on how to actually open an account (the documents you need, what to expect at the branch, the non-resident account process), see our complete guide to opening a Spanish bank account.

The Banks, Reviewed

BBVA
✓ Our Recommended Pick
💶 €0/month with conditions (3+ card transactions) 🌐 Full English app & website 🏦 ~3,200 branches in Spain

If you spend any time in Spain expat communities and ask which bank to use, BBVA comes up more than anything else — and it's not nostalgia or brand recognition. The bank has genuinely invested in its English-language digital offering. The mobile app is fully translated, customer support has English speakers, and the account opening process is more foreigner-friendly than most alternatives.

For non-EU residents, BBVA offers a Cuenta No Residente (non-resident account) that can be opened with a valid passport and proof of foreign address — no NIE required initially. Once you have your NIE in Spain, converting to a standard resident account is straightforward. The standard "Cuenta Online" waives its monthly fee if you make at least 3 card transactions per month, which essentially anyone with an active account will do naturally.

Their app includes Spanish tax filing links, direct debit management, and the usual digital banking features — all of it in English. For most people moving to Spain on an NLV, DNV, or student visa, BBVA is the path of least resistance.

Bottom line: BBVA is the default recommendation for English-speaking expats moving to Spain. Good app, flexible on documentation, genuinely international in how it approaches foreign customers.
CaixaBank
Largest branch network
💶 €0/month ("Smart Account" with conditions) 🌐 Good English app 🏦 ~4,500 branches across Spain

CaixaBank is the biggest bank in Spain by branch count, which matters more than you might think. If you're moving somewhere outside the major cities — a village on the Costa Blanca, a town in Andalusia, a smaller island — there's a decent chance CaixaBank is the only full-service bank with a local presence. And in smaller Spanish towns, there are still plenty of transactions that require a physical branch.

For property buyers especially, CaixaBank is frequently recommended. They have experience with non-resident mortgage applications, understand the process of buying property as a foreigner, and their branches tend to be staffed with people who have seen the whole expat lifecycle many times. The Smart Account (Cuenta Smart) is fee-free if you receive a regular income — suitable for pension recipients and employed expats.

The English-language app is solid, though it lags behind BBVA in terms of full translation. Phone support in English exists but can be inconsistent. In person at branches in expat-heavy areas, English-speaking staff are common.

Bottom line: CaixaBank is the strongest choice for expats buying property, living in rural Spain, or wanting the reassurance of the country's broadest physical branch network.
Santander Spain
Large network
💶 €0/month with conditions 🌐 Improving English support 🏦 ~1,900 branches

Santander is a name most British expats already know from home, which is why it appears in so many conversations about Spanish banking. The important thing to understand is that Santander UK and Santander Spain are legally separate entities — your UK account history doesn't automatically transfer, and you'll be starting fresh with the Spanish side.

That said, Santander Spain is a solid bank with a wide branch network and improving digital services. Their zero-fee account works if you receive regular income or maintain a minimum balance. Where Santander has a real advantage is for expats who want to manage international transfers between Spain and the UK — the process between Santander entities is typically smoother than between different banking groups. Worth considering if you're expecting regular pension transfers from the UK or managing money across both countries actively.

Bottom line: A reasonable choice, particularly for those with strong UK-Spain financial ties. Not the first recommendation if you're choosing purely on English-language service quality.
Openbank (Santander Group)
Genuinely fee-free
💶 €0/month, no conditions 🌐 English app available 🏦 100% digital — uses Santander ATM network

Openbank is Santander's fully digital subsidiary and it's worth understanding the distinction: where Santander Spain has monthly fee conditions, Openbank is genuinely free — no salary requirement, no minimum transactions, no conditions. You pay nothing to hold the account regardless of how you use it.

They also pay interest on their savings account (the "Cuenta de Ahorro"), which most traditional Spanish banks still don't do at meaningful rates. The app is clean and functional, ATM access works through the Santander network, and the account comes with a standard ES IBAN — important for Spanish direct debits and bureaucracy.

The limitation is that you essentially need to be in Spain with your NIE to complete the account opening — they don't have a proper non-resident route like BBVA or Sabadell. That makes them less useful for people who want to set something up before arriving. But for expats already in Spain, particularly digital nomads or anyone on the DNV who wants a zero-cost banking setup, Openbank is genuinely excellent.

Bottom line: The best option if zero fees are your priority and you're already in Spain with your NIE. The savings interest rate is a genuine plus. Not ideal for pre-arrival account opening.
Sabadell BS Online
Built for British expats
💶 €0/month 🌐 Full English service 🏦 Online-first; Sabadell branches for in-person needs

Sabadell's BS Online product was created specifically for the British expatriate market in Spain — it has English-language everything, staff who understand the British-to-Spain relocation process, and an online application designed for people who aren't yet in Spain when they apply. It's the most deliberately foreigner-focused product from a mainstream Spanish bank.

The account carries no monthly fee, accepts online applications from UK residents (making it one of the clearest options for opening before you move), and gives you access to the wider Sabadell branch network once you're in Spain. For anyone relocating from the UK on an NLV or coming to retire in Spain who wants everything sorted before the moving truck arrives, this is worth serious consideration.

That said, it's a somewhat niche product and doesn't have the same broad digital infrastructure as BBVA or Openbank. For people from outside the UK, the UK-centric design can feel slightly off — BBVA will likely serve you better.

Bottom line: The clearest choice for British expats, especially for pre-arrival account opening. Less relevant for non-British nationalities.
N26
Best as a supplement
💶 €0/month (standard) 🌐 Full English 🏦 German bank — DE IBAN

N26 is a German fintech bank with an excellent product. In most European countries it works brilliantly as a primary account. In Spain specifically, it creates friction that most expats find irritating over time: the IBAN starts with DE (German), not ES (Spanish), and Spanish utility companies, landlords, and government systems frequently reject non-ES IBANs for direct debits.

As a supplementary account it's very useful — particularly for digital nomads who move between countries and want a single account that works everywhere in the EU. But relying on N26 alone for life in Spain means fighting with Spanish bureaucracy every time you set up a new direct debit or deal with a public agency. Most expats who've tried it eventually open a Spanish bank account alongside it.

Bottom line: Excellent product, wrong IBAN for Spain as a standalone. Best used alongside a proper Spanish bank account.
Wise
Essential for transfers
💶 Low per-transfer fees; account holding is free 🌐 Full English 🏦 Multi-currency account — EU IBAN (not ES)

Wise isn't a bank in the traditional sense, and we shouldn't pretend it is. It doesn't offer credit, it isn't part of the Spanish banking system, and for Spanish bureaucracy purposes it doesn't replace a proper account. But almost every expat in Spain — including people with solid BBVA or CaixaBank accounts — also has Wise, and for one very clear reason: international money transfers.

If you're moving money from a UK, US, or Australian bank account into Spain, Wise will almost certainly give you a significantly better exchange rate and lower fees than your traditional bank. For pension recipients whose income arrives in sterling or dollars, Wise handles the currency conversion more efficiently than any Spanish bank will. You can hold multiple currencies, convert between them at mid-market rates, and receive funds from abroad with local bank details in the UK, US, EU, and many other countries.

Think of Wise as infrastructure rather than a bank. Use it for moving money, and keep a traditional Spanish account — BBVA, Openbank, or whichever suits you — for your actual day-to-day life in Spain.

Bottom line: Essential tool for handling international transfers, particularly for pension or salary income from abroad. Not a replacement for a Spanish bank account.

Which Bank Should You Choose?

The right answer depends on your situation more than any single ranking. Here's how we'd think about it:

Moving to Spain on an NLV
BBVA or CaixaBank
Either handles the non-resident phase well. BBVA's English app makes setup easier; CaixaBank suits you better if you're buying property or living outside a major city.
Digital Nomad Visa applicant
BBVA + Openbank
Start with BBVA for the non-resident phase, then add Openbank once you have your NIE — you get a fee-free account with savings interest and a clean ES IBAN for your tax obligations.
British expat arriving from the UK
Sabadell BS Online
The only mainstream option specifically built for British expats that lets you get properly set up before you leave the UK.
Buying property in Spain
CaixaBank
The largest branch network means more in-person support for mortgage applications and property-related banking, including in smaller Spanish towns.
Receiving pension from abroad
BBVA or CaixaBank + Wise
Use Wise to receive and convert your foreign pension at mid-market rates, then transfer into your Spanish account. Far cheaper than doing it through the bank directly.
Student visa holder
BBVA or Openbank
Both are fee-free with normal usage and work fine for student-level financial activity. Openbank pays interest if you can keep a balance between your parents' transfers.

Can You Open a Spanish Bank Account Before You Arrive?

The short answer is: partly. Sabadell BS Online has the most straightforward remote opening process for British residents. BBVA allows a significant portion of the process online, though you'll typically need to verify documents in person at a branch within a set period. Openbank can be started digitally but largely requires you to be in Spain with your NIE to complete.

If opening before arrival is important to you, Sabadell BS Online is the clear choice if you're from the UK. For other nationalities, BBVA is usually the most accommodating for getting the process started. In practice, most expats open their Spanish account in the first few days after arrival — once you have your NIE appointment confirmed and your rental contract in hand, the bank visit tends to happen naturally.

Non-Resident Bank Accounts: What You Need to Know

A cuenta no residente (non-resident account) is designed for foreigners who need a Spanish bank account but don't yet have residency established. They typically require a passport and proof of foreign address rather than an NIE or TIE, and they have some limitations — often you can't set up direct debits, and you won't have access to credit products.

The main use cases are: buying property in Spain before your visa is granted, or managing financial obligations in Spain from abroad (tax payments, for example). BBVA and Sabadell are the most accessible options for non-resident accounts. Most people transition to a standard resident account once they arrive and have their NIE issued — the process is usually as simple as presenting your NIE at the branch.

Worth noting: Spanish banks are required to verify the source of funds for non-resident accounts, particularly for large deposits or property purchases. This is standard FATF compliance, not something specific to Spain, but it's worth having documentation ready for anything beyond routine transactions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best bank in Spain for expats?
For most English-speaking expats, BBVA is the standout choice — a genuine English-language app, flexible documentation requirements, and a non-resident account option. Openbank wins on fees (genuinely zero, no conditions). British expats relocating from the UK should look at Sabadell BS Online, which was purpose-built for that market.
Can I open a Spanish bank account without an NIE number?
Yes — BBVA and Sabadell both offer non-resident accounts that can be opened with a passport and foreign proof of address. Once you obtain your NIE in Spain (which happens after you arrive), you'd update the account to a standard resident account. Openbank and most others require an NIE to complete the full account opening.
Do I need a Spanish bank account for the Non-Lucrative Visa?
A Spanish bank account is not a formal requirement for the NLV application — you prove your financial means through home country bank statements or investment accounts. Once you're in Spain, however, you'll need one almost immediately: rental deposits, utility direct debits, NIE/TIE registration fees, and daily life all require local banking. Most NLV holders open a Spanish account as one of their first tasks after arrival.
What documents do I need to open a bank account in Spain as a foreigner?
For a non-resident account: valid passport and proof of your home address. For a standard resident account: passport, NIE or TIE number, and proof of Spanish address (rental contract or utility bill). Some banks also ask for proof of income or employment, though this varies. BBVA and CaixaBank tend to be the most straightforward for foreigners.
Can I open a Spanish bank account online before moving?
Sabadell BS Online has the clearest remote opening process for UK residents — it was designed for that purpose. BBVA has a digital application process but may require in-person verification within a time window. Openbank needs you to be in Spain with your NIE. If opening before arrival is a priority, Sabadell is your best bet if you're from the UK; BBVA otherwise.
Is Revolut or N26 enough as a standalone bank account in Spain?
For most expats in Spain, no — and this catches people off guard. Spanish utility companies, landlords, and government agencies frequently reject non-ES IBANs for direct debits. N26 gives you a German IBAN, Revolut a Lithuanian one, and these regularly fail with Spanish systems. Both are excellent supplementary accounts (especially for international transfers), but most Spain-based expats end up maintaining a proper Spanish account alongside them.

DIGITAL NOMAD GUIDE

Digital Nomad Visa Lanzarote: Working Remotely on a Volcanic Island

Lanzarote is not Gran Canaria. It does not have the same density of co-working spaces, the same nomad meetup scene, or the same urban energy. What it has is extraordinary landscape, good internet, very low costs, and the Canary Islands' genuine fiscal advantages. For the right kind of nomad, that is a better deal.

Honest assessment: If you want a buzzing nomad hub with co-working options every few streets, nomad events every week, and a large community of fellow remote workers, choose Las Palmas de Gran Canaria instead. If you want a remarkable, affordable, legally sound base in the Canary Islands with good internet and real fiscal advantages — and you are comfortable working independently — read on.

The Canary Islands have attracted more attention from remote workers since Spain introduced its Digital Nomad Visa in 2023. Gran Canaria, and specifically Las Palmas, gets the most press — it has been building nomad infrastructure for years. But Lanzarote offers something that Las Palmas cannot: a landscape and quality of life that is genuinely unusual, at a cost that makes comfortable living very achievable.

This guide gives you an honest picture of what nomad life in Lanzarote is actually like — the infrastructure, the gaps, the fiscal argument, and what the DNV actually requires.

Who Lanzarote Works For as a Nomad Base

Be clear-eyed about this. Lanzarote suits nomads who:

  • Work independently and do not depend on a local co-working community for productivity or social connection
  • Value landscape, outdoor lifestyle, and visual environment over urban stimulation
  • Want a stable, affordable long-term base rather than rotating between cities
  • Are interested in the Canary Islands' fiscal advantages and want to understand them properly
  • Are comfortable with the reality of island life — limited transport, car required, everything at a slightly slower pace

It suits less well if you need regular access to co-working communities, depend on nomad networks for client acquisition, or find quiet landscapes mentally isolating. That is not a failure of Lanzarote — it is just a mismatch of preferences.

Puerto del Carmen: The Best Nomad Base

If you are working in Lanzarote, Puerto del Carmen is the obvious choice for a base. It has the highest density of English-speaking infrastructure, the best selection of cafés and restaurants that tolerate laptop workers, the largest number of rental options, and direct access to the island's best beaches.

Arrecife (the capital) is an alternative for those who want a more Spanish urban environment — there are co-working options there, and the atmosphere is less resort-like. But for most nomads, Puerto del Carmen balances practicality with liveability more effectively.

Costa Teguise works for those who want a quieter base with access to the island's windsurf and cycling infrastructure. Playa Blanca is too quiet for most nomads — beautiful, but not designed for daily working life.

Internet Connectivity

Internet infrastructure in Lanzarote has improved substantially. Fibre is now available across Puerto del Carmen, Costa Teguise, Arrecife, and Playa Blanca through providers including Movistar, Vodafone, and local operators. Speeds of 300–600 Mbps are standard on good fibre connections.

A few practical notes:

  • When renting, ask specifically whether the building has fibre or ADSL — older buildings in some areas still rely on ADSL, which is slower and less reliable.
  • 4G mobile coverage from Movistar, Orange, and Vodafone is solid across the main inhabited areas and provides a reliable backup.
  • Remote areas of the island (inland volcanic areas, some coastal spots) have weaker signal. This matters less if you live in a resort area.
  • A personal router with a SIM card as a backup is standard nomad practice and costs very little in Spain.

Co-Working Spaces

Lanzarote's co-working provision is limited. This is the honest truth. There are functional spaces in Arrecife and in Puerto del Carmen — enough that you can find a proper desk if you cannot work productively from home. But these are working spaces, not community hubs. Do not expect a curated nomad environment with regular events, introductions, and a community manager.

What does exist:

  • Co-working spaces in Arrecife (the capital) — the most developed provision on the island
  • A small number of co-working-friendly café environments in Puerto del Carmen that accommodate laptop workers reasonably well
  • Short-term desk rental options in some shared offices

Most nomads in Lanzarote work primarily from home (or a rented apartment with good fibre) and treat co-working spaces as an occasional alternative rather than a daily routine. If your productivity depends on a dedicated co-working environment with guaranteed gigabit speeds and a community, budget for this accordingly or choose a different base.

Cost of Living

This is where Lanzarote's argument for nomads is strongest. The Canary Islands' lower tax regime (IGIC at 7%) means costs are measurably lower than mainland Spain, which is itself cheaper than northern Europe. A nomad living comfortably in Lanzarote spends significantly less than in Barcelona, Madrid, or Palma.

Monthly costs for a single nomad

  • Rent, one-bedroom apartment with fibre (Puerto del Carmen): €650–€900
  • Groceries: €200–€280
  • Utilities (electricity, water, internet included in most rentals): €80–€130
  • Private health insurance (DNV requirement, age 25–40): €60–€100
  • Car hire or ownership (highly recommended): €200–€400
  • Dining out, leisure: €250–€400
  • Total: approximately €1,450–€2,250 per month

Compare this to Las Palmas (€1,800–€2,800), Barcelona (€2,500–€3,500), or London (£3,500+). The cost advantage is real and meaningful at the level of annual savings.

Canary Islands Fiscal Advantages

For nomads with the Digital Nomad Visa who qualify, the Canary Islands offer two significant fiscal advantages:

IGIC at 7%

The Canary Islands' local tax (IGIC) is 7% compared to mainland Spain's VAT of 21%. This applies to most goods and services — groceries, restaurants, building work, most consumer purchases. Over a year of living in the Canary Islands, the difference accumulates noticeably.

ZEC (Zona Especial Canaria)

Qualifying businesses registered under the ZEC pay corporate tax at 4% rather than the standard 25%. To qualify, you need to register a limited company in Spain, meet certain employment and investment conditions, and operate in an eligible sector. This is not relevant for solo freelancers operating under their own name, but it is a real advantage for nomads who are running or considering running a Spanish company.

The Beckham Law (RESA)

The Régimen Especial de Impatriados — known as the Beckham Law — allows qualifying DNV holders to pay a flat 24% tax rate on Spanish-sourced income up to €600,000 per year, rather than Spain's progressive rates that reach 47%. To qualify, you must not have been a Spanish tax resident in the five years prior to arrival. The regime applies for six years from the year of arrival and must be formally elected within six months of registering as a Spanish tax resident.

Combined with the IGIC advantage, the total fiscal position for nomads in Lanzarote — particularly higher earners — can be significantly better than in most of Europe. Get proper tax advice specific to your circumstances before making decisions on this basis.

The Digital Nomad Visa: Requirements

The Spain Digital Nomad Visa (DNV) was introduced in 2023 and is the correct legal route for remote workers who want to live in Spain long-term. Key requirements for 2026:

Income

  • Minimum 200% of the Spanish minimum wage (SMI): approximately €2,300 per month in 2026
  • Income must be from remote employment with a company registered outside Spain, or from self-employment serving clients primarily based outside Spain
  • No more than 20% of your income may come from Spanish clients
  • Dependent family members require an additional 75% of SMI per person

Documents required

  • Proof of remote work (employment contract or freelance contracts demonstrating foreign client base)
  • Evidence of income: recent payslips, bank statements, or invoicing records
  • Private health insurance covering Spain with no co-payments
  • Clean criminal record certificate (apostilled)
  • Proof of accommodation in Spain
  • Completed application forms and passport photographs

Timeline

The DNV is applied for at your Spanish consulate before you move. Processing typically takes four to eight weeks. Once granted, it is valid for one year initially, then renewable for two-year periods. After five years of legal residence, long-term residency is available.

Life in Lanzarote as a Working Nomad

If the numbers and visa requirements work for you, what does the daily reality look like?

Most nomads settle into a rhythm of working mornings from home or a café, then afternoons that make use of the island's extraordinary environment — the beaches, the volcanic national park (Timanfaya), the coastal paths, the water sports. Surfing is limited compared to Fuerteventura (calmer waters on the eastern side), but diving, snorkelling, cycling, and hiking are all excellent. The island is small enough that you can drive the entire coast in an afternoon.

The Manrique-designed environment — no neon signs, no high-rises, whitewashed buildings with volcanic stone — gives Lanzarote a visual coherence that is easy to underestimate until you have lived somewhere that lacks it. Some nomads find this calm sustaining. The absence of urban stimulation is the other side of that coin.

English is widely spoken in the main resort areas, which eases daily life considerably. The island has a large established British community — not primarily of nomads, but of long-term residents and retirees — whose infrastructure (English-speaking solicitors, accountants, doctors) is available to nomads as well.

The main practical challenges: you need a car, public transport is minimal, and the island is small. After six months, you will have driven every road. Some people find that deeply peaceful. Others find the circuit tightens. Know yourself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Lanzarote good for digital nomads?
Lanzarote works well for nomads who prioritise lifestyle and scenery over co-working community density. It is not a nomad hub like Las Palmas de Gran Canaria. What you will find is reliable fibre internet, very affordable costs, a stunning volcanic landscape, and the Canary Islands' favourable tax regime. For nomads who work independently and value a visually extraordinary environment, it can be an excellent base.
What internet speed can I expect in Lanzarote?
Fibre internet is well-established in Puerto del Carmen, Costa Teguise, Arrecife, and Playa Blanca. Speeds of 300–600 Mbps are typically available. When renting, confirm fibre availability in the specific apartment — older buildings sometimes still have ADSL. 4G mobile coverage is reliable across the island as a backup.
Are there co-working spaces in Lanzarote?
There are a small number — functional spaces in Arrecife and options in Puerto del Carmen. These are working spaces rather than community hubs. Most nomads in Lanzarote work primarily from home with good fibre and use co-working spaces occasionally. If you need a buzzing co-working community, Las Palmas de Gran Canaria is the better Canary Islands choice.
What income do I need for the Digital Nomad Visa in Spain?
The DNV requires a minimum income of 200% of the Spanish minimum wage — approximately €2,300 per month in 2026. Income must come from remote employment with a foreign company or self-employment serving clients primarily based outside Spain. No more than 20% of your income can come from Spanish sources.
What is the Beckham Law and does it apply to Lanzarote?
The Beckham Law (RESA) allows qualifying DNV holders who were not Spanish tax residents in the prior five years to pay a flat 24% tax rate on Spanish-sourced income up to €600,000, rather than progressive rates of up to 47%. It applies throughout Spain including the Canary Islands, and must be formally elected within six months of registering as a Spanish tax resident.
What are the Canary Islands tax advantages for digital nomads?
IGIC at 7% (versus mainland 21% VAT) reduces everyday living costs meaningfully. The ZEC special economic zone offers 4% corporate tax for eligible registered businesses. Combined with the Beckham Law flat rate, the Canary Islands can be a genuinely tax-efficient base for international remote workers — particularly higher earners.
